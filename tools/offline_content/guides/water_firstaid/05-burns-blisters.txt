Burns and Blisters

Most burns you can manage yourself with water, clean cover, and
fluids. Do that first. Going for help is one option among the steps
below, not the whole plan. The hard exceptions are named honestly at
the end.

== DO THIS FIRST: STOP AND COOL ==
- Stop the burning. Put out flames, get off the heat source.
- Chemical burn: brush off any dry powder, then flush with lots of
  water for many minutes. Acid and alkali keep eating until removed.
- Electrical burn: make sure the current is off before you touch the
  person. Damage runs deep and is easy to underestimate.
- Take off rings, watches, and tight clothing near the burn before
  swelling starts.
- Do not peel off cloth stuck to the skin. Cool over it, leave it.
- Cool the burn with cool, clean running water for 20 minutes. This
  still helps up to 3 hours after the burn and can keep the burn from
  getting deeper. Poured or trickled water counts if none is running.
- Never put ice or snow directly on a burn. Extreme cold kills more
  tissue and deepens the injury. You can use snow to chill your water,
  just not against the skin.
- Big burn: cool the burn but keep the rest of the person warm and
  dry. Watch for shivering. Stop cooling if the whole body chills.

== HOW BAD IS IT ==
- Superficial (first degree): red, dry, painful, like sunburn. Heals
  on its own in a few days.
- Partial thickness (second degree): blisters, wet shiny surface, very
  painful. Heals over weeks with clean care.
- Full thickness (third degree): white, leathery, brown, or charred.
  May not hurt in the center because the nerves are gone. If large, it
  will not close over on its own.
- Size it with the palm. The casualty's palm plus fingers is about 1
  percent of their body. Use it to judge how much skin is burned.

== COVER AND PROTECT ==
- After cooling, cover loosely. Best options: a non-stick dressing,
  clean plastic cling wrap, or the cleanest cloth you have.
- Cling wrap is ideal on burns because it does not stick. Lay it on,
  do not wrap it tight around a limb (swelling needs room).
- Do not pop burn blisters. Intact skin is a sterile dressing.
- Do not apply butter, grease, oil, or toothpaste. They trap heat and
  feed infection.
- Plain aloe or moisturizer is fine on superficial, unbroken burns
  only. Not on open blisters or deep burns.

== NO WAY OUT: KEEP A BURN CASUALTY ALIVE ==
For a large burn with no evacuation, this is the core of survival.

Fluids (this is what kills first in a big burn):
- Burns leak huge amounts of body fluid. Anyone burned over more than
  about their whole arm must drink steadily or slide into shock.
- Make a rehydration drink: 1 liter clean or treated water, 6 level
  teaspoons sugar, one half level teaspoon salt. Stir until dissolved.
  Measure carefully. Too much salt does harm. Sports-drink powder plus
  a pinch of salt also works.
- How much: for a large burn aim for about 1 liter per hour, taken as
  small sips every few minutes. Small sips beat big gulps, less
  vomiting.
- Drinking can carry someone through burns up to roughly a third to
  40 percent of the body. Past about half the body, fluids by mouth
  cannot keep up, but keep giving them anyway. It is still the best
  chance you have.
- Gauge it by urine. Peeing a pale color at regular intervals means
  you are keeping up. Dark or no urine means push more fluid.

Wound care over the days that follow:
- Change the dressing about once a day. Rinse the burn gently with
  clean or treated water. Pat the skin around it dry.
- Honey is your best improvised burn dressing. Plain honey spread on
  the burn under the cover has real evidence for fighting infection
  and speeding healing in burns. Use it when you have no antibiotic
  cream. Reapply at each change.
- If you carry antibiotic ointment or silver burn cream, use that.
  If not, clean plus honey plus a clean cover is the plan.
- Keep the burned part raised above heart level when you can to limit
  swelling.

Pain:
- Ibuprofen and acetaminophen, if you carry them and they are safe for
  the person. They can be alternated for a stronger effect together.
- Elevation and a snug, not tight, dressing also cut pain.

== THE HARD CASES: THE TRUTH ==
Some burns have no field fix. Do not chase a fake cure. Do the real
thing below and move toward help if any help exists.

- Burn all the way around a limb or finger, full thickness: as it
  swells, the stiff dead skin can choke off blood like a tourniquet.
  Watch the hand or foot past it. Cold, white, numb, or no pulse means
  it is losing circulation. The only true fix is a surgical release
  (escharotomy), which is not a safe backcountry procedure. Raise the
  limb, take off anything constricting, and get to care. This is a
  reason to go now if you can.
- Airway or inhalation burn (fire in a closed space, singed nose
  hairs, soot in the mouth, hoarse voice, coughing black material):
  the throat can swell shut over minutes to hours. There is no field
  cure for a closing airway. Keep the person sitting upright, calm,
  and still. Cool humid air helps a little. Do not let them lie flat.
  This one kills fastest. If evacuation exists, use it immediately.
- Plain truth: deep burns over large areas, and the two cases above,
  need a hospital to survive well. Good field care buys time and
  comfort. It does not replace surgery, IV fluids, and skin grafting.

== EVACUATE IF YOU CAN SAFELY REACH HELP ==
Do the cooling, covering, and fluids first. They stand on their own.
Move toward care, when it is reachable, for:
- A burn bigger than the casualty's palm, or any full-thickness burn.
- Burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals, or all the way around a
  limb.
- Any electrical or chemical burn (deeper than it looks).
- Any airway signs above.
- Infection you cannot get on top of.

== BLISTERS: STOP THEM EARLY ==
A hot spot (rubbing warmth or a sting) is a blister forming. Stop the
moment you feel one. Ten minutes now saves a trip-ending wound.
- Cover the hot spot with tape or moleskin before a blister forms.
- Smooth sock wrinkles, shake out grit, dry your feet, re-lace.
- Damp feet blister fast. Change or dry socks at breaks.

== BLISTERS: DRAIN OR PROTECT ==
An intact blister roof is the best dressing there is. Protect it when
you can.
- Small blister, not on a weight-bearing spot: pad around it and leave
  it alone.
- Large, tense, or painful blister you must walk on, or one blocking a
  joint you need to bend to keep moving: draining it is reasonable.
  - Clean the skin. Heat a needle in a flame until it glows, let it
    cool.
  - Puncture at the base, the lowest edge, so gravity drains it.
  - Roll the fluid out like a toothpaste tube. Leave the roof in place.
- Never cut away or peel off the roof.
- Never drain blood-filled blisters, or blisters from burns or
  frostbite. Cover and protect those.

== MOLESKIN AND TAPE ==
- Cut a doughnut of moleskin, hole slightly larger than the blister.
  Stick it around, not on, the blister. Stack a second doughnut if it
  still takes pressure.
- Cover the hole with tape, or a dab of ointment on the roof, so the
  dressing does not stick to it.
- Round the corners of every tape piece so it does not peel. Apply to
  clean, dry skin.

== IF A WOUND OR BURN GETS INFECTED ==
Deep in the backcountry, catching it early is on you.
- Signs: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks running
  up the limb, fever, or pain that keeps getting worse.
- Do: soak off the dressing, wash out with clean or treated water,
  apply honey or antibiotic cream, re-cover, keep it raised. Repeat
  once or twice a day.
- Take oral antibiotics if you carry them and know the dose.
- Red streaks, fever, or fast spreading despite care point to a blood
  infection. There is no reliable field cure for that. It is a strong
  reason to evacuate if you possibly can.
