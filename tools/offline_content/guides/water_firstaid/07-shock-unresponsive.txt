Shock and the Unresponsive Person

Shock and unresponsiveness are signs the body is failing. Field care supports the casualty; it does not fix the cause. Treat what you can (stop the bleeding, give epinephrine for anaphylaxis) and start working on evacuation early.

== RECOGNIZING SHOCK ==
Shock means the body cannot circulate enough blood. Backcountry causes: bleeding you can see, bleeding you cannot, severe dehydration, anaphylaxis, major injury.

- Rapid, weak pulse; rapid shallow breathing.
- Pale, cool, sweaty skin; blue lips or nail beds.
- Anxiety, restlessness, unusual thirst, confusion.
- Late and grave: drowsiness and fading responses.

== POSITIONING AND INSULATION ==
- Lay the casualty flat on insulation. The ground steals heat fast; get a pad, pack, or boughs underneath and wrap over the top. A cold casualty in shock does worse.
- Raise the legs 6 to 12 inches if there is no leg, pelvis, or spine injury and breathing is fine.
- If breathing is hard, let them semi-sit if the spine is not suspect.
- Nothing to eat. Small sips of water only if fully alert and help is far away.

== MONITORING ==
- Every 5 to 15 minutes check: response level (alert, responds to voice, responds to pain, unresponsive), breathing rate, pulse, skin color.
- Write it down with times. The trend matters more than any single check, and rescuers will want the record.

== THE UNRESPONSIVE PERSON ==
Shout and squeeze the shoulder. No response: check breathing for up to 10 seconds, watching the chest.

- Breathing normally: recovery position, keep warm, monitor.
- Not breathing, or only occasional gasps: start CPR.

== RECOVERY POSITION ==
An unresponsive person on their back can choke on their tongue or vomit. The recovery position protects the airway.

- Kneel beside them. Place the near arm out at a right angle. Bring the far hand against their near cheek. Bend the far knee up, then roll them toward you onto their side.
- Tilt the head back slightly, mouth pointed downhill.
- Recheck breathing every few minutes. If waiting hours, swap sides about every 30 minutes.

== COMPRESSION-ONLY CPR (ADULT) ==
Be honest with yourself: CPR keeps blood moving until a defibrillator and hospital care arrive. Far from rescue, survival from cardiac arrest is very unlikely. Start anyway when the story favors it: cold-water drowning, lightning strike, avalanche burial, or a collapse you witnessed. It is acceptable to stop when you are exhausted, the scene turns unsafe, or after about 30 minutes with no sign of life. Exception: a severely hypothermic casualty is not dead until warm and dead; continue and push for rescue.

- Kneel over the center of the chest. Heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, other hand on top, arms straight.
- Push hard and fast: at least 2 inches deep, 100 to 120 pushes per minute, letting the chest fully rebound each time.
- Swap rescuers every 2 minutes; quality drops fast with fatigue.

== PROTECTING THE SPINE ==
Suspect a spine injury after a fall from height, a high-speed impact, a blow to the head, or if there is neck or back pain, numbness, or tingling.

- Hold the head steady in line with the body. Do not twist or bend the neck.
- If you must move or roll them, move head, shoulders, and hips as one unit, one person controlling the head.
- Airway beats spine: if an unresponsive breathing casualty may vomit, a careful, aligned roll into the recovery position is the right call.

== GO FOR HELP OR STAY ==
If the group is big enough, send two people with written details: location or coordinates from this device, the casualty's condition, and the time. If you are alone with the casualty:

- Stay if they need constant care (airway, CPR, bleeding), the weather is dangerous, or someone knows your plan and rescue is likely.
- Go if nobody knows you are out here and there is no way to signal. Before leaving: stop any bleeding, insulate them, leave water in reach, mark the spot visibly, and note your route.

Decide early, in daylight, before exhaustion decides for you.
