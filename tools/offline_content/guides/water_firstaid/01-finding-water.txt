Finding Water

Dehydration degrades judgment within hours and can kill within days. Ration sweat, not water: travel in the cool hours, rest in shade, breathe through your nose. If you have water, drink it. Do not hoard it while you get weaker.

== READ THE TERRAIN ==
Water flows downhill and collects at low points. Move downhill and let the land guide you.

- Follow drainages, gullies, and valley bottoms downhill. Even dry ones lead toward water.
- Look for lines of green: cottonwoods, willows, cattails, and unusually lush brush mark water at or near the surface.
- Animal trails that converge and point downhill often lead to water, especially at dawn and dusk.
- Grain-eating birds fly low and straight toward water in the morning and evening. Bees rarely range far from water.
- In a dry creek bed, dig at the outside of bends and at the lowest, shadiest point. Damp sand may seep enough to collect over an hour.
- Check rock depressions and potholes, especially on shaded north-facing slopes.

== DEW ==
- Before sunrise, drag absorbent cloth through grass or tie cloth around your ankles and walk, then wring it into a container.
- Slow and low-yield, but low effort and usually clean.

== RAIN ==
- Rig a tarp or poncho as a funnel: weight the center with a small stone so it sags over your container.
- Rain caught directly in clean material is among the safest water you can get without treatment.

== SNOW ==
Warning: do not eat snow. Melting it in your mouth drains core body heat, pushes you toward hypothermia, and yields very little water for the effort.

- Melt snow first: over a flame, in a dark container in the sun, or in a bottle carried between clothing layers (not against bare skin).
- Put a little liquid water in the pot before adding snow; it melts faster and avoids a scorched taste.
- Prefer ice over snow when you can get it. Ice gives far more water per volume.

== TRANSPIRATION BAGS ==
- Tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch of a healthy, non-toxic tree or shrub in full sun. Seal the opening tightly and let one corner hang low.
- Water condenses over several hours. Expect small amounts, often under a cup per bag per day, so set several.
- Never bag poisonous plants such as poison ivy, poison oak, or oleander.

== MYTHS TO AVOID ==
- Cactus is not a canteen. Barrel cactus pulp is bitter and alkaline and commonly causes vomiting and diarrhea, which dehydrate you faster. Do not cut into cactus for water.
- Never drink urine, seawater, or alkaline pond water. All of these make dehydration worse.
- Ground solar stills usually cost more water in sweat digging them than they return. Treat them as a last resort, not a plan.

== PRIORITIZE SOURCES BY CONTAMINATION RISK ==
From safer to riskier:

- Rain and dew collected in clean material.
- Springs taken right at the source; fast, cold headwater streams.
- Large lakes, drawn away from shorelines and inlets.
- Slow rivers, beaver ponds, and stagnant pools: highest risk, always treat and prefilter.

Avoid water near dead animals, with algae scum or a chemical sheen, or downstream of camps, mines, and grazing land. Whatever you collect, treat it if you have any way to do so. See the treating water guide.
