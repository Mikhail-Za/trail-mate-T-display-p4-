Bites and Stings

Antivenom, rabies shots, and antibiotics only exist at a hospital. For serious bites and stings, everything you do in the field is about buying time and moving toward care.

== SNAKEBITE: THE MODERN PROTOCOL ==
In North America, most venomous bites are from pit vipers: rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths.

Do NOT:
- Cut the wound or try to suck out venom, by mouth or with a kit. It does not work and causes harm.
- Apply a tourniquet or tight band.
- Apply ice.
- Give alcohol.
- Chase or handle the snake for identification. A photo from a safe distance is plenty; treatment is the same either way.

Do:
- Move everyone away from the snake.
- Keep the casualty calm and as still as possible. Exertion spreads venom faster.
- Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts.
- Splint or immobilize the bitten limb and keep it at about heart level.
- Draw a line around the swelling edge and write the time. Repeat as it spreads; this tells doctors how fast it is moving.
- Evacuate to a hospital as fast as safely possible. If walking out slowly is the only way, walk out slowly.

Some bites inject no venom, but never assume that. Every snakebite gets evaluated.

== SPIDER BITES OF CONCERN ==
- Black widow: pinprick bite, then within an hour cramping muscle pain in the abdomen, back, or limbs, with sweating. Rarely fatal to healthy adults but severe: evacuate for care.
- Brown recluse (mainly the South and Midwest): often painless at first, then a blister and a dark, sunken sore over days. Clean it, cover it, seek care.
- Most suspected "spider bites" are actually skin infections. Either way, watch for spreading redness and fever.

== BEES, WASPS, AND ANAPHYLAXIS ==
- Scrape a bee stinger out sideways with a fingernail or card as fast as possible. Do not pinch it; that squeezes in more venom.
- Cold compress and an oral antihistamine handle normal local pain and swelling.

Anaphylaxis is a killer and moves in minutes. Watch for: hives spreading beyond the sting, swelling of lips, tongue, or face, tight throat, wheezing, dizziness, vomiting, collapse.

- Give epinephrine (auto-injector) immediately into the outer thigh; through clothing works. If in doubt, give it. Epinephrine is the treatment; nothing else substitutes.
- Give a second dose after 5 to 15 minutes if not clearly improving.
- Antihistamines come after epinephrine, never instead of it.
- Lie the person flat with legs raised unless breathing is easier sitting.
- Evacuate even if they recover. Symptoms can return hours later.

== TICKS ==
- Remove ticks as soon as found. With fine-tipped tweezers, grip the tick right at the skin, by the head, and pull straight up with steady pressure.
- No twisting, burning, petroleum jelly, or nail polish. Those make the tick regurgitate into you.
- Wash the site and note the date.
- Watch for 30 days: an expanding rash (a bullseye ring is classic, but any growing red patch counts), fever, chills, headache, or body aches. Any of these means seek care and mention the tick. Early antibiotics treat Lyme and most other tick diseases well.

== ANIMAL BITES ==
- Every mammal bite is a dirty wound with high infection risk, cat and human bites worst of all. Irrigate hard with lots of clean water, do not close the wound, dress it, and get professional cleaning and antibiotics.
- Rabies: a bite or scratch from a bat, raccoon, skunk, fox, coyote, or any unknown dog or cat needs prompt medical attention. Rabies is essentially always fatal once symptoms start, and the post-exposure shots work only if started early.
- Any direct contact with a bat, or waking up with a bat in your tent or shelter, counts as a possible exposure. Get seen.
