Bleeding and Wounds

Severe bleeding can kill in minutes. Act immediately; do not spend time looking for perfect supplies. If you have gloves, put them on, but do not delay for them. Any deep wound, puncture, or animal bite needs professional care even if you control it in the field.

== DIRECT PRESSURE ==
This stops most bleeding.

- Expose the wound. Cut or pull clothing away so you can see the bleeding point.
- Press hard directly on the bleeding point with gauze or the cleanest cloth you have. Use the heel of your hand and lean your body weight into it.
- Hold continuous pressure for at least 10 minutes by the clock before you look. Peeking early tears the forming clot loose.
- If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top. Do not remove the first layer.
- Once controlled, wrap a snug bandage over the dressing to hold pressure. Check that fingers or toes beyond it stay pink and warm; loosen slightly if they go numb or blue.

== WOUND PACKING ==
For deep wounds in the groin, armpit, or neck-shoulder junction, or when surface pressure fails:

- Pack gauze (hemostatic gauze if you have it) deep into the wound, directly against the bleeding vessel, filling the whole cavity.
- Then press hard on top: 10 minutes with plain gauze, 3 minutes with hemostatic gauze.
- Do not pack wounds of the chest or abdomen. Cover those and press.

== TOURNIQUET: LIFE OVER LIMB ==
Use a tourniquet when a limb is spurting or pooling blood fast, when direct pressure fails, or when you cannot keep pressure on (you must move, or you are alone). Modern evidence: a proper tourniquet is safe on a limb for hours. Fear of losing the limb should never stop you from saving the life.

- Apply 2 to 3 inches above the wound, not over a joint.
- Tighten until the bleeding stops and the pulse beyond it is gone. It will hurt badly. That is normal and expected.
- Write the time on the tourniquet or the casualty's skin.
- Do not loosen or remove it in the field. That is a hospital decision.

Improvised tourniquet cautions: use a band at least 1.5 inches wide (folded bandana, cravat) with a rigid windlass (stout stick) twisted tight and tied in place. Never use cord, wire, or shoelaces; narrow bands cut flesh and fail to stop deep bleeding.

== CLEANING WOUNDS IN THE FIELD ==
After bleeding is fully controlled:

- Irrigate with the cleanest water available, ideally water safe to drink. Volume and pressure matter: use a liter or more, squirted from a squeeze bottle, syringe, or a pinhole in a bag.
- Pick out visible dirt and debris.
- Do not pour alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, or iodine solution into the wound; they damage tissue. Use antiseptics only on intact skin around it.

== CLOSE OR LEAVE OPEN ==
- In the field, leave wounds open. Closing a dirty wound traps bacteria and breeds infection.
- Cover with a clean non-stick layer and a bandage. Change it daily or when soaked.
- Only small, clean, well-irrigated cuts may be pulled together with tape strips.
- Gaping wounds, punctures, bites, and anything dirty must stay open and be closed by a professional.

== INFECTION SIGNS TIMELINE ==
- First 24 hours: some redness and soreness at the edges is normal.
- 24 to 72 hours: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, throbbing pain, or pus means infection is starting.
- Red streaks running from the wound toward the body, fever, or chills mean spreading infection. This needs antibiotics. Evacuate now, not tomorrow.
