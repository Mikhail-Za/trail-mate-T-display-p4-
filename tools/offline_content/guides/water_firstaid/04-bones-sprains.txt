Fractures and Sprains

You often cannot tell a bad sprain from a fracture in the field. When in doubt, treat it as a fracture. Never force a limb to move to "test" it. An open fracture (bone through skin), a limb that is cold, blue, or numb below the injury, or a suspected pelvis, thigh, or spine injury is an emergency: keep the casualty still and get help.

== WHEN TO SUSPECT A FRACTURE ==
- A crack or pop was heard or felt, with rapid swelling.
- The limb looks deformed, bent where it should be straight, or shortened.
- Pain is pinpoint on the bone itself, or there is a grating feel with movement.
- The casualty cannot bear weight for four steps, or cannot use the limb at all.

All suspected fractures need an X-ray and professional care, even if they start to feel better.

== SPRAINS: RICE ==
- Rest: stop using the joint. Get off it.
- Ice: cool it with snow or cold water wrapped in cloth, about 20 minutes at a time. Never put ice or snow directly on skin.
- Compression: wrap snugly with an elastic bandage, from below the joint upward. Toes or fingers must stay pink and warm.
- Elevation: raise it above heart level whenever resting.

Most mild sprains can bear careful weight the next day. If it cannot, treat it as a fracture.

== SPLINTING PRINCIPLES ==
Before and after splinting, check circulation, sensation, and movement below the injury: skin color, warmth, feeling, wiggle.

- Splint the limb in the position found. Do not straighten a deformed limb unless it is pulseless and help is many hours away.
- Immobilize the joint above and the joint below the injury. For a forearm, that means wrist and elbow.
- Pad everything: bony bumps, hollows, the whole contact surface. A splint that hurts will not be tolerated.
- Tie the splint on above and below the injury, never directly over it.
- Recheck circulation right after splinting and every 15 to 30 minutes. If the hand or foot goes pale, cold, or numb, loosen the ties.

== IMPROVISED SPLINTS ==
- A foam sleeping pad rolled into a U-channel around the limb is the best all-around backcountry splint.
- Sticks, tent poles, or trekking poles padded heavily with spare clothing work for legs and forearms.
- Buddy-tape an injured finger or toe to its neighbor with padding between them.
- For arm, shoulder, or collarbone: sling the arm with a triangle bandage, then bind it gently against the chest (sling and swathe).

== ANKLE TAPING BASICS ==
For a sprain the casualty must walk on:

- Keep the boot on if swelling allows. A laced boot is itself a splint. Once removed, a swelling foot may not go back in.
- Hold the ankle at 90 degrees. Run tape stirrups: start on the inside of the lower leg, pass under the heel, pull up the outside. Lay several overlapping strips.
- Add a figure-eight around the ankle and midfoot to lock it. Snug, not tight; toes stay pink.

== WHEN THE CASUALTY MUST NOT WALK ==
Do not let them walk with:

- Suspected fracture of the thigh, hip, or pelvis, or any suspected spine injury.
- Any leg injury where the foot below is numb, cold, or pulseless.
- Obvious deformity of the leg bones.

Splint gently, insulate them from the ground, watch for shock, and send for or signal rescue. Carrying or dragging a casualty over rough ground is a last resort; a bad carry can make the injury worse.
