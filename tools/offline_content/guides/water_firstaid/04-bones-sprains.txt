Fractures and Sprains

You may be days from help, or unable to move at all. Assume no one is
coming and that this is on you. Your job is not to "set" the bone the way
a hospital would. It is to keep blood flowing past the injury, stop it
getting worse, control bleeding and pain, keep out infection and cold,
and keep the limb as usable as you can. Every step below stands on its
own. Evacuate later only if you can safely reach help.

You often cannot tell a bad sprain from a fracture in the field. When in
doubt, treat it as a fracture. Never force a limb to move just to test it.

== FIRST MOVES, ANY BONE OR JOINT INJURY ==
Do these in order:
- Stop major bleeding first. Direct pressure; a tourniquet high and tight
  only for spurting, unstoppable limb bleeding.
- Check below the injury: skin color, warmth, feeling, ability to wiggle.
  This is CSM (circulation, sensation, movement). Note it as a baseline.
- Strip rings, watch, tight clothing off the limb before it swells.
- Support the limb in the position it is comfortable in.
- Get the casualty off cold ground and keep them warm and dry.
- Give pain medicine early (see SHOCK, COLD, PAIN).

== WHEN TO SUSPECT A FRACTURE ==
- A crack or pop was heard or felt, with rapid swelling.
- The limb looks deformed, bent where it should be straight, or shortened.
- Pain is pinpoint on the bone itself, or grating is felt with movement.
- The casualty cannot take four steps on it, or cannot use it at all.

A break you cannot X-ray still gets managed the same way in the field:
align if needed, splint, protect, watch the circulation.

== CIRCULATION FIRST: REALIGN A DYING OR BADLY BENT LIMB ==
If the hand or foot below is cold, blue, numb, or pulseless, OR the limb
is badly angled and you cannot reach help soon, bring it back toward
normal shape. A straighter limb bleeds less, hurts less, and splints
better. This is standard wilderness practice when care is far off.

How:
- Grip below the break and pull slow and steady straight along the length
  of the limb (in-line traction) while someone steadies above the break.
- Ease it back toward straight and to normal length. Rough is fine. You
  are not aiming for perfect.
- Keep gentle tension while you apply the splint, then hold it there.
- STOP if you hit firm resistance or pain spikes hard. Splint it where it
  is. Do not crank on it.
- Do not haul on a joint itself to "straighten" it. A popped-out joint is
  a dislocation, handled below.
- Recheck CSM. Return of warmth, color, and feeling means you succeeded.

Honest note: you cannot truly set or heal a bone out here. Aligned and
splinted is the goal. Some deformity or a bad long-term result is
acceptable next to losing the limb or bleeding out.

== SPLINTING: RIGID, PADDED, AND WATCHED ==
Check CSM below the injury before and after you splint.

- Splint in the position found, or in the aligned position if you pulled
  it straight. Support the natural curve.
- Immobilize the joint above and the joint below the break. For a
  forearm that means wrist and elbow.
- Pad everything: bony bumps, hollows, the whole contact surface. A
  splint that hurts will not be kept on.
- Make it stiff enough that the break cannot move.
- Tie above and below the injury, never directly over it.
- Recheck CSM right after splinting and every 15 to 30 minutes. If the
  hand or foot goes pale, cold, or numb, loosen the ties at once.

== IMPROVISED SPLINTS ==
- A foam sleeping pad rolled into a U-channel around the limb is the best
  all-around backcountry splint.
- Sticks, tent poles, or trekking poles padded heavily with spare
  clothing work for legs and forearms.
- Buddy-tape an injured finger or toe to its neighbor, padding between.
- For arm, shoulder, or collarbone: sling the arm with a triangle
  bandage, then bind it gently to the chest (sling and swathe).

== DISLOCATIONS: PUT IT BACK, SOONER IS EASIER ==
A joint popped out of place chokes its own blood and nerve supply. The
longer it stays out, the more the muscles spasm and the harder it is to
put back. Reducing it early, gently, is a real field skill.
- Check CSM first. Get the casualty relaxed. Pain medicine helps.
- Move slowly. Muscles must relax for the joint to slide home. You will
  often feel or hear a soft clunk when it goes.
- STOP if it will not go after a couple of gentle tries, or if pain or
  resistance climbs. Splint it as it lies and protect the limb.
- Recheck CSM after. A reduced joint usually feels dramatically better
  and can often be used gently.

Shoulder (most common), try any of these:
- Hanging traction (Stimson): casualty lies face down with the arm
  dangling off a ledge or pack. Hang 10 to 15 pounds from the wrist
  (a water bag, a rock in a sack). Wait 20 to 30 minutes and let it
  relax back in.
- External rotation: elbow bent and held against the side, slowly rotate
  the forearm outward like opening a gate.
- Cunningham: casualty sits upright, chest out, arm relaxed at the side;
  massage the shoulder and upper arm muscles until they let go.

Kneecap (patella, usually slid to the outside):
- Slowly straighten the knee while pushing the kneecap gently back toward
  the midline. It usually pops in on its own as the leg straightens.

Finger or toe:
- Steady pull along the length of the digit with a counter-hold on the
  hand or foot, then ease it back into line. Buddy-tape or splint after.

== SPRAINS: PROTECT AND LOAD ==
The old "RICE" advice to rest completely is outdated. Gentle early use
heals a mild sprain faster. The current approach:
- Protect it from a second injury.
- Optimal loading: once the worst pain eases, put careful weight or use
  on it as pain allows. Let pain be the limit. Do not baby it into
  stiffness, but do not force it either.
- Compression: wrap snugly with an elastic bandage from below the joint
  upward. Toes or fingers must stay pink and warm.
- Elevation: raise it above heart level when resting.
- Cooling is optional and only for comfort and swelling the first day:
  snow or cold water wrapped in cloth, about 20 minutes at a time. Never
  put ice or snow directly on skin.

Most mild sprains take careful weight the next day. If it cannot, or if a
complete tear is likely (total instability), splint and treat it as a
fracture.

== ANKLE TAPING TO WALK OUT ==
For a sprain the casualty must walk on:
- Keep the boot on if swelling allows. A laced boot is itself a splint.
  Once off, a swelling foot may not go back in.
- Hold the ankle at 90 degrees. Run tape stirrups: start on the inside of
  the lower leg, pass under the heel, pull up the outside. Lay several
  overlapping strips.
- Add a figure-eight around the ankle and midfoot to lock it. Snug, not
  tight; toes stay pink.

== OPEN FRACTURE: BONE THROUGH SKIN ==
The wound, not the bone, is the danger. Infection over days is what kills.
- Stop bleeding first with pressure around, not on, the bone end.
- Rinse the wound with clean, disinfected water to float out visible dirt.
  Do not scrub deep and do not deliberately shove the bone back in.
- If you pull traction and the bone slips back under the skin on its own,
  that is fine. Cover it.
- Cover with the cleanest moist dressing you have.
- Splint the limb.
- If you carry antibiotics, start them as soon as possible. Update tetanus
  when you reach care.

Honest note: cleaning lowers the infection risk but does not remove it.
Without antibiotics and surgical cleaning, a serious open-fracture
infection is likely within days. This is one of the strongest reasons to
evacuate if you possibly can. If you cannot, keep irrigating with clean
water, keep it covered, and watch for spreading redness, pus, foul smell,
and fever.

== THIGH FEMUR FRACTURE: IT CAN BLEED YOU OUT ==
The big thigh muscles around a broken femur can hide over a liter of
blood loss. This can be fatal on its own.
- Apply gentle in-line traction, pull the leg out to length, and keep
  that tension held.
- Improvise a traction splint, or bind the injured leg to the good leg
  with a padded pole or rolled pad between them, feet and knees tied.
- Treat aggressively for shock. Keep the casualty warm.

Honest note: this bleeding may need surgery to truly stop. Traction,
warmth, and shock care buy time. They are not a guaranteed fix.

== PELVIS AND HIP ==
Suspect a broken pelvis after a hard fall or crush, with pain when the
hips are gently squeezed, or an inability to stand.
- Wrap a binder around the widest part of the hips, over the hip knobs
  (greater trochanters), NOT up on the belt-line bones. Use a folded
  sheet, jacket, or pack strap. Snug it firm.
- Tie the feet together and the knees together to close the pelvis in.
- Do not rock or spring the pelvis over and over to test it.
- Do not tie leg-to-leg traction on a broken pelvis.

Honest note: pelvic bleeding can be lethal and usually needs a hospital.
The binder can slow internal bleeding and buy time. It is a time-buyer,
not a cure.

== SPINE AND NECK ==
Suspect a spine injury after a hard fall, a blow to head, neck, or back,
or if there is neck or back pain, or numbness, tingling, or weakness in
the limbs.
- Keep the head, neck, and back in one neutral straight line.
- Pad around the head and body to stop it rolling. Move the whole body as
  a single unit if you must move at all.
- If you MUST move to survive, out of water, off a cliff, out of the cold,
  keep the spine in line and move slow and controlled.

Honest note: you cannot fix a broken spine or fully rule one out in the
field. There is no field cure. Minimize movement, prevent cold, and get
skilled help if it exists. If it does not, careful positioning and time
are all you have.

== COMPARTMENT SYNDROME: A LIMB EMERGENCY ==
Watch for it after a bad break or crush, even under a good splint:
- Pain far worse than the injury seems to warrant.
- Sharp pain when you gently stretch the toes or fingers.
- A tight, hard, swollen limb, and creeping numbness.
- A missing pulse is a late, unreliable sign. Do not wait for it.

Act: loosen every tie, bandage, and splint. Remove all outside pressure.

Honest note: true compartment syndrome needs a surgical release within
hours to save the limb. In the field you can only take off outside
pressure. If this is happening, evacuate as fast as you can.

== SHOCK, COLD, AND PAIN ==
Cold makes bleeding and shock worse. Fight it hard.
- Insulate the casualty from the ground; keep them warm and dry.
- Lay them flat, legs raised slightly, ONLY if there is no leg, pelvis,
  or spine injury.
- Give small sips of water if they are fully awake and have no belly
  injury. Add food and warmth.

Pain control:
- Ibuprofen 400 to 600 mg every 6 to 8 hours WITH food (up to about 2400
  mg in 24 hours). Skip ibuprofen if there is heavy bleeding or the
  casualty is badly dehydrated.
- Acetaminophen 1000 mg every 8 hours (no more than 3000 to 4000 mg in
  24 hours).
- The two can be given together for stronger relief.
- The single best painkiller is a well-padded, rigid splint. Splint first.

== EVACUATE OR STAY ==
Evacuate if, and only if, you can safely reach help. That is one option
among the actions above, not the whole answer. If no help is reachable,
the field management above is your care.

Do NOT let the casualty walk with:
- Suspected fracture of the thigh, hip, or pelvis, or any spine injury.
- A leg injury where the foot below is numb, cold, or pulseless.
- Obvious deformity of the leg bones.

A laced boot is a splint, and a walkable ankle sprain can self-rescue.
Carrying or dragging a casualty over rough ground is a last resort; a bad
carry can make the injury worse. Splint well, insulate from the ground,
watch for shock, and signal for rescue if you have any means to.
