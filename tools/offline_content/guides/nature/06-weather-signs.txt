Reading Weather Without a Forecast

== CLOUD PROGRESSION: THE CLASSIC WARM FRONT TIMELINE ==
- High wispy cirrus ("mares' tails") drifting in: moisture arriving aloft. A front MAY be 24 to 48 hours out, especially if the wisps thicken rather than fade.
- Cirrostratus: thin milky sheet that dims the sun and makes halos. Often 12 to 24 hours before precipitation.
- Altostratus: thicker gray sheet, sun visible only as a dull disk. Rain or snow commonly within about 12 hours.
- Nimbostratus: low, dark, featureless deck. Steady precipitation is beginning or imminent.
- The convective ladder is different: puffy fair-weather cumulus that starts TOWERING vertically by late morning can become cumulonimbus by afternoon. Dark flat bases, cauliflower tops, and an anvil top mean a thunderstorm is effectively here.
- Watch trend, not snapshots. Clouds lowering and thickening through the day = deteriorating. Lifting and breaking = improving.

== FALLING PRESSURE: WHAT YOU CAN FEEL ==
- Campfire and chimney smoke hangs low, sinks, or curls downward instead of rising straight: pressure is falling and rain is likelier. Smoke rising straight and high suggests fair weather.
- Smells get stronger before rain: swamp, soil, and plant odors release more readily in low pressure and high humidity. "The ditch smells today" is real data.
- Birds and bats fly low before storms, following insects that stay low in heavy humid air. Birds sitting quietly in trees mid-morning can also signal a coming blow.
- Sound carries farther and sharper before bad weather (a low cloud deck reflects it). Distant trains or highways sounding unusually close is a hint.

== MOUNTAIN AFTERNOON STORMS ==
- In summer mountains, sun heats slopes all morning, air rises, and thunderstorms fire over peaks most afternoons, often on a near schedule (roughly noon to 4 pm).
- Plan around it: start ridge and summit pushes at dawn and be OFF exposed terrain, peaks, ridgelines, lone trees, open water, by early afternoon.
- First warning signs: cumulus towers building over peaks by 10 or 11 am mean an early and active storm day. Descend sooner.
- If caught: get below treeline, spread the group out, crouch on your pack away from tall trees. Hair standing up or buzzing rocks means strike danger NOW.

== HALO AROUND SUN OR MOON ==
- A ring around the sun or moon is ice crystals in cirrostratus, the leading edge of moisture aloft.
- Rough rule: precipitation follows within about 24 hours a good share of the time. Not a guarantee, but combined with thickening cloud and a south or east wind it is a strong signal.

== RED SKY RULE ==
- "Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky at morning, sailors take warning."
- Physics: mid-latitude weather generally moves west to east. Low sun lights the sky through hundreds of miles of air, and dry dusty air scatters a deep red.
- Red EVENING sky means the light is coming through clear dry air to your WEST, i.e. the weather arriving next. Red MORNING sky means the clear air is to your east, already leaving, while the reddened clouds overhead may be a system arriving from the west.
- Limits: works in the mid-latitude westerlies; unreliable in the tropics and when local terrain drives the weather.

== WIND SHIFTS AND FRONTS ==
- Steady wind from one direction usually means steady weather. A definite shift often marks a front.
- Common cold front signature: wind swings from south or southwest around to west or northwest, with gusts, a temperature drop, and often a sharp line of showers, then clearing, colder, drier air.
- Warm moist south or southeast wind that strengthens while clouds lower usually means rain is coming.
- Sudden calm and an odd green-gray or yellow cast to the sky during a hot afternoon can precede severe thunderstorm or hail; take shelter early.
