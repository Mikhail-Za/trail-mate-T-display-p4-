Reading Animal Tracks

== CANINE VS FELINE ==
- Canine (dog, coyote, wolf, fox): claw marks usually show; overall oval shape; symmetrical print. You can draw an X through the negative space between the toes and the heel pad.
- Feline (bobcat, cougar, house cat): claws retracted, so normally NO claw marks; round overall shape; a leading toe makes the print asymmetrical. The negative space forms a C or arc, not an X, and the heel pad has two lobes in front, three behind.
- Size ladder: house cat about 1 inch; bobcat about 2 inches; cougar 3 to 4 inches wide. Coyote about 2.5 inches long; wolf 4 to 5 inches, noticeably bigger than almost any dog.
- Caution: loose dogs make the most common "wolf" track. Check for human boot prints alongside.

== HOOVED: DEER, ELK, MOOSE ==
- All leave two-toed, heart-shaped prints; the point of the heart faces the direction of travel.
- Deer: 2 to 3.5 inches long, narrow and pointed.
- Elk: 3 to 4.5 inches, rounder and blockier than deer, like a deer print inflated.
- Moose: 4.5 to 7 inches, long and pointed, often with dewclaw marks behind in mud or snow. In deep substrate any of these can show dewclaws.

== BEAR ==
- Five toes on BOTH front and hind feet (canines and felines show four).
- Front track: short and wide, like a broad pad with a toe row. Hind track: long, remarkably like a flat human footprint with claws.
- Black vs grizzly: on a clear print, grizzly toes sit in a nearly straight line close together with claw marks well ahead of the toes; black bear toes form a more curved arc with claws close to the toes. Grizzly fronts commonly exceed 5 inches wide.

== RACCOON AND FRIENDS ==
- Raccoon: unmistakable little "hands," five long finger-like toes on front and hind, usually paired left-front beside right-hind. Common along water and around camps.
- Opossum: hand-like too, but the hind foot shows a thumb sticking out sideways.
- Skunk: five toes with long straight foreclaws from digging.

== GAIT PATTERNS ==
- Walkers (deer, cats, canines): even zigzag line of single prints; cats and foxes often direct register, hind foot landing exactly in the front print, making a neat single line.
- Bounders (weasels, mink, otter): paired prints side by side in a line of two-by-two hops.
- Gallopers (rabbits, squirrels): larger hind feet land AHEAD of the front feet. Squirrel front feet pair side by side; rabbit front feet land one behind the other.
- Waddlers (bear, raccoon, porcupine, skunk): wide-bodied shuffle, prints paired and offset.

== AGING A TRACK ==
- Fresh: crisp sharp edges, moist pressed soil darker than surroundings, undisturbed fine detail, no debris inside.
- Aging: edges crumble and round off, the floor dries and lightens, wind drops leaves and dust in, insect tracks cross it.
- Weather is your clock: a track on top of last night's rain or frost is newer than that event; a rained-in, softened track is older.
- Snow: fresh tracks have sharp walls; sun and wind round them and enlarge them, making old tracks look deceptively big.

== TRACKS TO WATER ==
- Game trails are a network; where several converge and widen, they usually lead somewhere valuable, and downhill convergence often means water.
- Follow the heavier, more braided trail downhill, especially in dry country. Fresh tracks of many species stacked together suggest a shared water source.
- Grain and seed eating birds (doves, finches) fly low and straight toward water around dawn and dusk; erratic flight usually means they have already drunk.
- Insects also mark water: bees range within a few miles of it, and columns of gnats or dragonflies suggest it is close.
