Other Dangerous Wildlife

== MOOSE ==
- Moose injure more people in North America each year than bears do. Never treat one as tame.
- Highest risk: cows with calves in spring, bulls in the fall rut, and any moose harassed by dogs or deep snow.
- Warning signs of a charge: ears pinned back, hackles (long hair on hump and neck) raised, head lowered, lip licking or smacking, swaying, urinating.
- Give moose far more room than feels necessary, 50 yards or more. If one blocks the trail, wait or detour widely. Do not try to push past.
- If it charges: RUN. Unlike with bears and cougars, running from a moose is correct. Get behind a tree, boulder, or car; moose will rarely circle an obstacle for long.
- If knocked down, curl up, protect your head, and stay still until it moves well away. Getting up too soon can restart the stomping.

== COUGAR (MOUNTAIN LION) ==
- Attacks are rare; most cougars avoid people. Small solo hikers, runners, and children are at highest risk.
- If you see one: stop. Never run and never turn your back; running triggers the chase instinct.
- Maintain eye contact, stand tall, open your jacket, raise your arms or trekking poles, and speak loudly and firmly. Look big, not like prey.
- Pick up small children immediately without bending low or crouching.
- Back away slowly while facing the cat. Give it a way out.
- If it approaches, throw rocks and sticks. If it attacks, FIGHT BACK hard; aim for the eyes and face and protect your neck. People regularly drive cougars off this way.

== COYOTES AND WOLVES ==
- Attacks on adults are very rare. Bold behavior usually means the animal has been fed by humans.
- Haze bold coyotes: yell, wave arms, throw objects near them, advance a step. Do not run.
- Keep dogs leashed; a loose dog can lure coyotes or wolves back to you.
- Wolves: stand tall, back away slowly, make noise. Never run. Fight back if one makes contact.
- Report any canid that follows people persistently or shows no fear; that is abnormal.

== RACCOONS, SKUNKS, AND RABIES ==
- Raccoons, skunks, foxes, and bats are the main rabies carriers in North America. Rabies is essentially 100 percent fatal once symptoms start, and fully preventable with prompt shots.
- Suspect rabies in any of these animals that is active in daytime, staggering, unafraid, or aggressive without cause.
- Never feed, corner, or handle them, including "orphaned" babies. Never touch a bat with bare hands.
- Any bite or scratch from a wild mammal, or waking up with a bat in your shelter: wash the wound hard with soap and water for 15 minutes and get medical care promptly for post-exposure treatment.

== READING AGGRESSION IN GENERAL ==
- Common warning signs across species: ears flattened back, raised hackles, hard direct stare, bared teeth, huffing or growling, ground pawing or hoof stomping, and short bluff charges.
- Defensive animals want distance: they warn first. Give them space and a clear exit and most encounters end.
- Predatory behavior looks different: silent, low, circling, cutting off your line of travel, following. That animal is testing you; get big, get loud, group up, and prepare to fight back.
- Never position yourself between any animal and its young, its food, or its escape route.
- An animal that keeps approaching after you back off is the exception, not the rule. Treat it as a serious threat.

== SEASON ==
- Moose are most dangerous around cows with calves in spring and early summer, and bulls during the fall rut; deep winter snow also shortens their temper.
- Cougars, coyotes, and wolves are a year-round concern, with bolder encounters in late winter and spring when food is lean and young are being raised.
- Rabies has no season; stay alert to odd daytime behavior in raccoons, skunks, foxes, and bats in any month, most often reported in spring and summer.
