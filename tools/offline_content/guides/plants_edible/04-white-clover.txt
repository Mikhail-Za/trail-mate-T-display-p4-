White Clover (Trifolium repens)

== WHERE ==
- Lawns, pastures, meadows, and trail edges across nearly all of North America.
- Leaves spring through fall; flowers late spring through summer, often again in fall.

== IDENTIFY ==
- Growth: creeping stems that run along the ground and root at the nodes, forming low mats.
- Leaves: three oval leaflets, most marked with a pale V-shaped or crescent watermark.
- Leaflet edges: very finely toothed; look closely.
- Flowers: round white to pale pink pom-pom heads of many tiny pea-type flowers, each head on its own leafless stalk; faint sweet smell.
- Taste check (only after all other features match): mild and green, never sour. A sour lemon taste means you have wood sorrel instead.

== LOOKALIKES ==
- No deadly lookalikes in its usual lawn and pasture habitat, but confirm anyway.
- Wood sorrel: heart-shaped leaflets creased down the middle and a sour taste; edible in its own right (see its entry) but a different plant.
- Black medic and hop clovers: similar leaves but small yellow flowers.
- Sweet clover (Melilotus): a tall, upright plant with spikes of small white or yellow flowers, not a creeping mat. Important because spoiled or moldy sweet clover develops a potent blood-thinning toxin. Never eat moldy or fermented clover of any kind.

== EDIBLE PARTS AND PREPARATION ==
- Flower heads: the best part. Eat a few fresh, or dry them for a pleasant tea. Use only fresh white heads; skip brown, wilted, or damp-stored ones.
- Leaves: small amounts raw; easier to digest briefly boiled or steamed.

== CAUTION ==
- Raw clover in quantity can cause bloating and gas; cooking or steeping avoids most of it.
- Eat only fresh, sound material. The mold rule above is the one hard safety line with clovers.
- People with strong legume allergies should treat clover with the same caution as other beans and peas.
- As always, avoid sprayed lawns.
