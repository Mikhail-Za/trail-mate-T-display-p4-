Staghorn Sumac (Rhus typhina)

== WHERE ==
- Dry, sunny ground: roadsides, field edges, rocky slopes, and clearings, mainly in eastern and central North America. The very similar smooth sumac extends the range across much of the continent.
- Red berry cones ripen mid to late summer and stay usable into fall.

== IDENTIFY ==
- Growth: a shrub or small tree, 6 to 20 feet, spreading by root into open colonies.
- Twigs: young branches are thick and covered in dense velvet fuzz, like a deer's antler in velvet. (Smooth sumac lacks the fuzz but matches everything else.)
- Leaves: pinnately compound, 11 to 31 lance-shaped leaflets with TOOTHED edges; the whole plant turns brilliant red in fall.
- Fruit: a dense, UPRIGHT, cone-shaped cluster of fuzzy DEEP RED berries at the branch tips.
- Habitat is a feature: dry and sunny, never standing in a swamp.
- Taste check, only after everything above matches: a licked berry cluster tastes tart and lemony.

== LOOKALIKES ==
- Poison sumac: causes severe rash, worse than poison ivy, but is easy to rule out on three independent counts.
- Habitat: poison sumac grows ONLY in swamps, bogs, and very wet ground. Staghorn grows on dry ground.
- Berries: poison sumac hangs loose, DROOPING clusters of WHITE to gray-green berries. Staghorn carries tight, upright RED cones. The no-white-berries rule alone rejects it.
- Foliage: poison sumac has 7 to 13 leaflets with SMOOTH, untoothed edges on hairless, often red-tinged stalks. Staghorn leaflets are toothed and its twigs fuzzy.
- Tree of heaven (Ailanthus): similar compound leaves, but the leaflets are nearly smooth-edged with a gland tooth or two at the base, crushed foliage smells like rancid peanut butter, and the seeds are papery wings, never red fuzzy cones. Not food.

== EDIBLE PARTS AND PREPARATION ==
- Sumac-ade: break two to four ripe red cones into COOL water, crush and swish, steep 15 to 60 minutes, then strain through tight cloth to remove the fine hairs. Sweeten if you can.
- Dried berries, rubbed off and sieved, make the tart red spice of Middle Eastern cooking.
- Harvest before long heavy rains; rain washes the sour malic acid off the berries.

== CAUTION ==
- Use cool water only; hot water pulls bitter tannins.
- Sumac is in the cashew family. People with cashew, pistachio, or mango allergies should avoid it.
- Always strain out the hairs, and never handle any sumac carrying white berries.
