Wood Sorrel (Oxalis species)

== WHERE ==
- Lawns, garden beds, woodland edges, path cracks, and open woods across most of North America.
- Spring through fall; lush again after rains.

== IDENTIFY ==
- Leaves: three HEART-shaped leaflets per leaf, each with a crease down the middle, folding along that crease in strong sun and at night.
- Taste (only after every other feature matches): a clean sour lemon tang from a nibbled leaflet. This sourness is the plant's signature.
- Flowers: five petals, most often yellow; some species pink to violet.
- Seed pods: tiny upright okra-shaped capsules on bent stalks.
- Growth: delicate, low, weak-stemmed clumps or sprawls; no watermark on the leaflets.

== LOOKALIKES ==
- Clover: the constant mix-up, and a harmless one. Clover leaflets are rounded ovals (not hearts), usually carry a pale chevron watermark, have no center crease, and taste green rather than sour.
- Black medic: clover-like with small yellow pea-type flowers; again rounded leaflets, no crease, no sour taste.
- No dangerously toxic plant matches the full combination of folded heart leaflets, sour taste, and okra-like upright pods. Confirm all of them anyway, every time.

== EDIBLE PARTS AND PREPARATION ==
- Leaves, flowers, and green pods raw as a trail nibble; a small handful steeped in cool water makes a lemonade-like drink.
- Light cooking is fine; it wilts to almost nothing, so it is best fresh.

== CAUTION ==
- The sourness is oxalic acid, the same family of compounds as in spinach and rhubarb stalks. Small, occasional amounts are fine for most people.
- Do not eat large quantities day after day, and avoid it with a history of kidney stones or kidney disease.
- Treat it as a seasoning and a morale plant, not a bulk food. The flavor naturally enforces the limit.
