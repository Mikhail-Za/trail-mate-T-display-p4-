Pine (Pinus species): needle tea and inner bark

== WHERE ==
- Pines grow across most of North America: dry forests, mountains, sandy plains, and plantations.
- Evergreen, so useful year-round; young spring needles make the best tea, and inner bark is a cold-season survival food.

== IDENTIFY ==
- The make-or-break feature: needles grow in BUNDLES of 2 to 5, and each bundle is wrapped at its base by a small papery sheath. Check several twigs.
- Smell: crushed needles smell strongly and unmistakably of pine resin.
- Cones: woody, with stiff spirally arranged scales.
- Bark: scaly or plated, often with sticky resin at wounds.
- Needles are long compared with most other evergreens, roughly 1 to 10 inches depending on species.

== LOOKALIKES ==
- YEW (Taxus): DEADLY, and this is the one to learn cold. Yew needles are flat, soft, and attached SINGLY in two rows along the twig, each on a tiny stalk. There are no bundles, no papery sheaths, no woody cones, and no pine smell. Instead, yew bears red berry-like cups (arils), each open at the end with a single dark seed visible. A small handful of yew needles or a few chewed seeds can kill an adult. Treat the entire plant as lethal. If the needles are single and flat, do not make tea.
- Spruce, fir, and hemlock trees also have single needles. Many people use them for tea, but they are outside this entry: if you cannot separate them from yew with total confidence, use only bundled-needle pines. (The hemlock TREE is unrelated to poison hemlock the herb, but the rule above keeps you safe without needing that distinction.)

== EDIBLE PARTS AND PREPARATION ==
- Needle tea: chop a small handful of fresh young needles, steep 5 to 10 minutes in hot water off the boil, strain. Good vitamin C.
- Inner bark: the moist whitish layer between outer bark and wood. Take it from storm-downed limbs, or one small vertical patch of a live tree; never cut a ring around a trunk. Edible raw in an emergency; better fried, boiled, or dried and ground into flour. Real survival calories.
- Pine pollen (spring): yellow catkin dust, mixed into food.
- Pine nuts: seeds of some species (notably pinyons); labor-intensive but calorie-dense.

== SEASON ==
- Needle tea: year-round from an evergreen, but soft young needles in spring make the mildest, most vitamin-rich brew.
- Pollen: spring, as a yellow dust shaken from the catkins.
- Inner bark: a cold-season survival food, best in late winter and early spring; work from storm-downed limbs when you can.
- Pine nuts: fall, as the cones open.

== CAUTION ==
- Pregnant women should avoid pine needle tea entirely. Ponderosa pine and some relatives cause abortion in cattle, and the risk is assumed to carry over.
- Strong turpentine taste means too many needles or too long a steep; keep it weak and moderate.
- Bark harvesting wounds trees and is restricted on most public land; prefer downed wood outside true emergencies.
