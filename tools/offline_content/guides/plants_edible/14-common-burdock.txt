Common Burdock (Arctium minus)

== WHERE ==
- Disturbed rich soil: barnyards, fencerows, trail edges, stream banks, and vacant lots across southern Canada and most of the United States.
- A biennial. Year one: a big basal rosette (the stage you want, summer through fall). Year two: a tall flowering stalk, then burs.

== IDENTIFY ==
- First-year rosette: very large heart-shaped leaves, up to arm length, with wavy edges, dull green on top and woolly GRAY-WHITE underneath.
- Leaf stalks: grooved and HOLLOW. Slice one across to check; this is a key test.
- Second-year plant: a stout stalk 3 to 6 feet tall with purple thistle-like flower heads that turn into round burs covered in hooked spines, the original Velcro, snagging on everything.
- Root: a long brown taproot, whitish inside, with a faint sweet, earthy smell.

== LOOKALIKES ==
- Rhubarb (a garden escape): its leaf BLADES are toxic. Rhubarb leaves are hairless and green underneath, and its stalks are SOLID and often red. Burdock is woolly-white underneath with hollow stalks. Check both features every time.
- Cocklebur: TOXIC, no part edible. Coarser plant with smaller, rough, sandpapery leaves; its burs are football-shaped with two stout beaks at the tip, unlike burdock's round, evenly hooked burs.
- Rosette rule: if the leaf is not a large heart with a woolly white underside on a hollow stalk, walk away. Several unrelated toxic plants (foxglove among them) also grow as fuzzy rosettes, but none pass all three checks.

== EDIBLE PARTS AND PREPARATION ==
- First-year root (no flower stalk on the plant): dig deep, scrub, peel, slice thin, and boil 20 minutes or through two changes of water. Nutty and substantial; cultivated in Japan as gobo. Real food value.
- Immature second-year flower stalk, cut before flowers open: peel off ALL the bitter green rind and boil the mild pith. Young leaf stalks can be treated the same way.
- The leaf blades themselves are intensely bitter and generally not worth eating.

== SEASON ==
- Harvest only first-year plants, the low rosette with no flower stalk; their roots are best dug late summer through fall, and again in early spring before the stalk shoots up.
- By the time the tall second-year flower stalk appears the root is woody and spent; take the peeled stalk pith instead, cut in spring before the flowers open.

== CAUTION ==
- Dig only first-year rosettes; second-year roots are woody and spent.
- The plant can irritate sensitive skin on contact, and the burs are a hazard to eyes and pets.
- Bitterness that survives peeling and boiling means stop; you either under-peeled it or you have the wrong plant.
