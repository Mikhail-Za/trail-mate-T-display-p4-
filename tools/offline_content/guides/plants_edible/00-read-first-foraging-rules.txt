READ FIRST: Foraging Rules

== THE PRIME RULE ==
- Never eat any wild plant unless you are 100 percent certain of its identity.
- Certainty means multiple independent features all match: leaf, stem, flower or fruit, smell, growth pattern, habitat, and season. One matching feature is not an identification.
- If any single feature does not match, the answer is no. "Probably" is a no. "It looks right" is a no.
- Hunger, cold, and fatigue push you toward wishful identification. Assume your judgment is degraded and demand more evidence, not less.

== WHAT THIS GUIDE IS ==
- These are text descriptions with no photos: a memory aid for plants you already know, not a primary identification tool.
- The right way to use it: learn these plants at home with photos, field guides, and ideally an experienced forager, before you need them.
- If you are meeting a plant for the first time through this screen, do not eat it.

== NO WILD MUSHROOMS. EVER. ==
- This guide contains no mushrooms, on purpose.
- Deadly mushrooms closely mimic edible ones, and the worst toxins destroy the liver a day or more after a symptom-free start. By the time you feel sick, it can be too late.
- Mushrooms are nearly calorie-free. Deaths outnumber the calories. The math never works. Skip them.

== FAMILIES AND COLORS TO AVOID ==
- Avoid the entire wild carrot/parsley family: umbrella-shaped flower clusters and ferny, divided leaves. Water hemlock is the deadliest plant in North America; a small taste of the root can kill, and it mimics edible relatives. Poison hemlock is nearly as dangerous. No plant in that family appears in this guide.
- Never eat white or yellow berries. Most are toxic and none are worth the gamble.
- Red berries are a mixed class; some are fine, some (yew) are deadly. Eat only specific red fruits fully identified in their own entries here.

== THE SAFEST BERRY CLASS ==
- Aggregate berries, the raspberry and blackberry form, are the safest wild berry class in North America: many small juice-filled beads clustered into a single berry on a thorny bramble.
- The rule applies only to that exact form. A single smooth berry gets no protection from this rule, whatever its color.

== START SMALL, ALWAYS ==
- Even with certain identification, eat a small amount first and wait several hours. Allergies are individual, and a new plant, region, or empty stomach can change how you react.
- Introduce one new plant at a time, prepared one way.

== THE UNIVERSAL EDIBILITY TEST (LAST RESORT ONLY) ==
- The test, honestly summarized: separate the plant into parts (leaf, stem, root) and test one part at a time. Hold it against your inner wrist, then your lip, then your tongue, then chew and hold without swallowing, waiting about 15 minutes at each step for burning, numbness, or itching. Then swallow a tiny amount and wait 8 hours with no other food. If nothing happens, eat a small spoonful and wait 8 more hours.
- Its limits: it takes more than 24 hours per plant part, ties you to camp, and does NOT protect against plants where a tiny dose is lethal or where damage is delayed, exactly the profile of water hemlock and the deadly mushrooms.
- It is a desperation tool for long-term survival, not a shortcut around identification. If you can eat only plants you know cold instead, do that.

== THE LAW ==
- Foraging is illegal or restricted in many parks, preserves, and public lands. Know the rules where you travel.
- In a genuine survival emergency, staying alive comes first. Sort out the paperwork after you are safe.

== CALORIES ALMOST NEVER MATTER ==
- In short-term survival, food is nearly last on the list. A healthy person can go weeks without eating; hours without warmth or a few days without water can kill. Spend your effort on shelter, water, and signaling first.
- Never take an identification risk for calories. Wild greens are vitamins and morale, not meals. A wrong plant can turn a survivable week into a fatal one.
