Lost or in Trouble: First Priorities

The moment you realize you are lost or hurt, the biggest danger is panic. People in trouble make things worse by rushing. Your first job is to stop moving and start thinking.

== STOP ==

- S - Stop. Sit down. Breathe slowly. Drink some water. Do not take another step until you have a plan.
- T - Think. How did you get here? When were you last certain of your location? Who knows your trip plan, and when will they raise the alarm?
- O - Observe. Check your gear, water, food, clothing, and this device's battery. Look at the terrain, the weather, and the daylight left.
- P - Plan. Choose one course of action based on what you actually have, not what you wish you had. Act only on the plan.

== RULE OF THREES ==

Use this to rank problems. In rough terms you can survive:

- 3 minutes without air. Airway, severe bleeding, and drowning risks come first.
- 3 hours without shelter in harsh conditions. Cold, wet, and wind kill faster than anything below.
- 3 days without water.
- 3 weeks without food.

Food is almost never the priority. Shelter and water almost always are.

== STAY OR MOVE ==

Staying put is usually the right call if any of these are true:

- Someone knows your route or return time.
- You are injured or exhausted, or night or bad weather is close.
- You have no confident idea which way safety lies.

Searchers find stationary people far faster than moving ones. Move only if nobody will miss you, you are in immediate danger where you are, or you can clearly see a road, trail, or building. If you move, leave notes and markers showing your direction.

== MARK YOUR LOCATION ==

Make yourself easy to find:

- Build markers visible from the air and ground: rock arrows, dragged lines in snow or sand, bright gear spread in the open.
- Stay near open ground where you can be seen, not deep under canopy.
- If you leave, mark your path every 50 to 100 feet and leave a note with your name, condition, direction, and time.

== CONSERVE BATTERY ==

- Send your position and situation early, while you have power.
- Then cut use: lower screen brightness, close extra apps, turn off radios you are not using.
- Keep phone and device warm, inside clothing near your body. Cold batteries die fast.
- Check for messages on a schedule, such as ten minutes every hour, instead of constantly.

== TELL RESCUERS WHERE YOU ARE ==

Any message, call, or text should lead with location:

- Coordinates from this device if you have them, read carefully, twice.
- Otherwise: last known point, trail name, direction and time traveled, and major features you can see, such as lakes, peaks, or drainages.
- Include your condition, party size, and supplies.
- Text messages often get through when calls cannot. Keep trying from high, open ground.

Once your location is out and your markers are up, your job is simple: stay warm, stay dry, stay visible, and stay put.
