Cold Injuries

Cold kills through two doors: hypothermia, the whole body cooling down, and frostbite, tissue freezing. Both are easier to prevent than to treat, and both are made far worse by wet clothing and wind.

== WET = DEAD ==

Water strips heat from the body about 25 times faster than air, and wet clothing keeps doing it for hours. In cold country, staying dry is not comfort, it is survival. Manage sweat as seriously as rain: slow down, vent layers, and strip a layer before a climb. If you get soaked in the cold, treat it as an emergency: get to shelter, get dry, get insulated now, not later.

Wind multiplies the loss. Wind chill describes how fast exposed skin loses heat; a 20 degrees F day with a 30 mph wind strips heat like a far colder calm day. Block the wind first. It is the cheapest heat you can buy.

== HYPOTHERMIA: STAGES ==

- Mild: intense shivering, cold hands and feet, and the "umbles": mumbles, stumbles, fumbles, grumbles. The person can still walk and talk, but judgment is already slipping.
- Moderate: violent shivering that later slows or stops, confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness, apathy. Shivering that stops while the person is still cold is a danger sign, not improvement.
- Severe: no shivering, barely conscious or unconscious, rigid, pulse weak or hard to find. This is immediately life threatening.

== HYPOTHERMIA: FIELD TREATMENT ==

- Stop. Get the person sheltered, out of the wind, and off the ground.
- Replace wet clothing with dry. If nothing dry exists, wring out what they have and add every dry layer available over it.
- Wrap the whole person, head and neck included, in sleeping bags, spare clothing, and an emergency blanket, with thick insulation underneath. Think burrito, not blanket on top.
- If they are alert and can swallow, give warm, sweet drinks and easy calories. Sugar plus water fuels shivering, which is the body rewarming itself. No alcohol.
- Do not rub or massage the skin, and do not put a severely hypothermic person against intense direct heat. Rough handling and sudden surface heat can trigger dangerous heart rhythms.
- Handle a moderately or severely hypothermic person gently, keep them flat, and treat evacuation as urgent. If they appear lifeless, still insulate them and call for rescue; very cold people have survived long resuscitations.

== FROSTBITE: DOS AND DON'TS ==

Frostbite usually hits fingers, toes, ears, nose, and cheeks. Early signs: numb, waxy, pale, or grayish skin that feels wooden.

Do:

- Get out of the wind and rewarm early. Superficial cold injury, frostnip, reverses if warmed quickly.
- Warm parts skin to skin: hands in your armpits, a partner's palm on your cheek, cold feet against a companion's belly under their clothing.
- Loosen boots and tight clothing, remove rings, and protect the part from freezing again.

Do not:

- Do not rub or massage frozen tissue, and never rub snow on it. Ice crystals shred the cells.
- Do not thaw a frozen part if it might refreeze. Refreezing does far more damage than staying frozen while you travel to care.
- Do not thaw with fires, stoves, or hot rocks. Numb tissue burns without warning.
- Do not walk on thawed feet unless there is no other way to reach safety.

== VAPOR BARRIER IMPROVISATION ==

A plastic layer can block evaporative and wind heat loss in an emergency. Plastic bags over dry socks, worn under a second sock or the boot, a trash bag worn as a vest under your shell, or an emergency blanket wrap all trap heat. Expect dampness inside; over a short emergency that trade is worth it. Check the skin regularly, and keep any plastic or foil layer away from open flame.
