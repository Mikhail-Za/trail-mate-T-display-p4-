Shelter

In cold or wet weather, shelter comes before water and long before food. A soaked, wind-chilled person can slide into hypothermia in hours, even at temperatures well above freezing. If conditions are bad, treat shelter as the emergency, not a comfort.

Start early. Building a shelter takes far longer than it looks, and doing it in the dark wastes energy and invites injury. If sunset is within two or three hours, start now.

== SHELTER SITE ==

Where you build matters as much as what you build. Avoid:

- Drainages, gullies, and dry washes. They funnel cold air at night and can flood fast.
- Deadfall hazards. Look up. Never build under dead trees, leaning trunks, or hanging broken limbs, called widowmakers.
- Ridgelines and open summits. Wind and lightning exposure.
- The very bottom of valleys, where cold air pools.

Look for flat, dry ground just above the valley floor, with a natural windbreak such as a rock face or dense standing timber, and building material close by.

== INSULATE FROM THE GROUND ==

The ground steals heat faster than the air. This is the most skipped and most important step. Before anything else, build a mattress: 4 to 8 inches of dry leaves, pine needles, grass, or boughs, thicker than looks necessary, because it compresses. Even sitting on a pack beats sitting on dirt.

== DEBRIS HUT ==

The debris hut is a one-person heat trap:

- Prop a strong ridgepole, about 9 feet long, on a stump, rock, or forked branch at hip height.
- Lean ribs of sticks along both sides, close together, angled steeply so rain sheds.
- Pile debris, leaves, needles, and bark over the frame at least 2 feet thick. Your fist pushed into it should not reach the ribs.
- Fill the inside with dry debris and burrow in feet first. Small is warm; you heat it with your body.
- Plug the entrance behind you with a pack or a plug of debris.

== LEAN-TO ==

Faster to build, and good with a fire:

- Lash or wedge a ridgepole between two trees at chest height.
- Lean ribs against one side, facing into the wind, and cover thickly with boughs, bark, or a tarp.
- Build your fire a safe step in front of the open side, with a rock or log wall behind it to push heat toward you.

== TARPS AND EMERGENCY BLANKETS ==

A tarp pitched low and tight as an A-frame or lean-to beats hours of debris work. Anchor the edges with rocks or logs if you lack cord. Use an emergency blanket as a roof liner, as a ground layer over debris, or wrapped around you inside the shelter. It blocks wind and reflects heat, but it is fragile and loud; keep it away from flame and sparks.

== SNOW ==

Snow is excellent insulation if you stay dry:

- A tree well, the sheltered pit around a big conifer trunk, makes a quick refuge. Line it thickly with boughs.
- Dig a simple snow trench and roof it with branches and snow blocks rather than attempting a snow cave, which takes skill and soaks you.
- Always keep a vent hole open, and never let your only entrance drift shut.
- Work slowly. Sweating in snow country wets your insulation and becomes a serious problem at night.
