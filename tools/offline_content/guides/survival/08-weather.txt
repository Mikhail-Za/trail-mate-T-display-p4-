Storm and Lightning Safety

Mountain and plains weather changes fast. Most storm casualties come from being caught in the open after ignoring an hour of warnings. Learn the warnings, act early, and give up altitude and objectives without hesitation.

== READING A BUILDING STORM ==

- Cumulus clouds growing tall and cauliflower shaped by late morning promise afternoon thunderstorms. Towers with dark, flat bases or an anvil shaped top mean a storm is already active somewhere.
- Watch for sudden temperature drops, gusty shifting wind, distant rumbling, or static: hair standing up, skin tingling, crackling sounds. These mean the storm is close and you are exposed.
- In summer mountains, plan to be off summits and ridgelines by noon. Storms build over the peaks first.

== THE 30-30 RULE ==

- Count seconds from a lightning flash to its thunder. Divide by 5 for distance in miles. Thirty seconds or less, about 6 miles, means lightning can reach you now: stop travel and get to safer ground immediately.
- Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before resuming exposed travel. Storms throw bolts from their edges and from behind their rain.

== WHERE NOT TO SHELTER ==

Warning: several obvious-looking shelters attract strikes or carry ground current.

- Lone trees, and the tallest trees in a stand. Standing under one is among the deadliest places to be outdoors.
- Ridgelines, summits, and open meadows where you are the highest point.
- Shallow caves, rock overhangs, and mine entrances. Ground current jumps the gap and passes through anything bridging roof and floor.
- Water, wet gullies, fence lines, and long metal objects. Get off lakes and out of the water early.

Safer choices: a low, uniform stand of smaller trees, or a dry depression in lower ground. A hard topped vehicle is good protection. A tent is no protection at all.

== LIGHTNING POSITION ==

If caught in the open with strikes close:

- Spread your group at least 50 feet apart so one strike cannot injure everyone, but keep sight of each other.
- Crouch on the balls of your feet, feet together, on a pack or sleeping pad if you have one. Feet together limits ground current entering one leg and exiting the other. Do not lie flat.
- Stow trekking poles away from you and stay off the radio during the worst of it.
- This position is a last resort, not protection. Its real lesson is to move early so you never need it.
- If someone is struck, they carry no charge and are safe to touch. Start CPR immediately if they are not breathing; lightning victims are often revivable.

== FLASH FLOODS ==

- A flood can arrive under blue sky from rain falling miles upstream. In canyon and wash country, the weather over the whole drainage is your weather.
- Never camp, rest, or park in a wash, a slot canyon, or any canyon bottom too narrow to climb out of quickly.
- Warning signs: rising or muddying water, floating sticks and debris, or a roar from upstream. Move up, not downstream. Even a few feet of elevation can save you. Abandon gear if it slows you.
- Never cross flowing water above your knees. Moving water is far stronger than it looks, and cold water disables you in minutes.

== WHITEOUT ==

When blowing snow or fog erases every reference:

- Stop. Walking in a whiteout produces circles, cliff edges, and cornice falls, not progress.
- Shelter in place: get out of the wind, insulate yourself from the snow, and protect your face. A snow trench or tree well works.
- Mark your position and keep the group physically connected: rope, cord, or hand to pack strap.
- Wait it out. Whiteouts end; falls do not. Move again only when you can see terrain or hold a reliable compass line over a short, known distance.
