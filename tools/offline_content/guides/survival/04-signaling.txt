Signaling for Rescue

Rescuers can only find what they can detect. Your job is to be louder, brighter, and stranger looking than the landscape. Signal early, signal in threes, and set up passive signals that keep working while you rest.

== THE RULE OF THREES ==

Three of anything is a recognized distress signal in North America:

- Three whistle blasts, a pause, then three more. A whistle carries much farther than your voice and costs no energy. Repeat at intervals; searchers move in and out of earshot.
- Three fires or three smoke columns in a triangle or a line, about 100 feet apart, only if you can build and control them safely.
- Three flashes, three shouts, three of almost anything, repeated.

If you hear a reply, such as a single blast or shout, answer and keep signaling so searchers can home in on you.

== SIGNAL MIRROR ==

On a sunny day a mirror flash can be seen for many miles, farther than any other simple signal:

- Any reflective surface works: a real signal mirror, a phone screen, foil, polished metal.
- To aim, hold the mirror near your eye. Stretch your other arm out and make a V with two fingers. Put the target, an aircraft, a vehicle, a distant ridge, in the V, then rock the mirror so the flash sweeps across your fingers and the target.
- Sweep the horizon now and then even when you see no one. Pilots report mirror flashes from people who never saw the aircraft.

== GROUND-TO-AIR SYMBOLS ==

Build symbols in open ground, big and high contrast:

- V means require assistance. X means require medical assistance. An arrow shows your direction of travel if you moved on.
- Make each stroke at least 10 feet long and 3 feet wide; bigger is better. Use logs, rocks, trenches stamped in snow, or gear that contrasts with the ground.
- Add contrast and shadow: pile material high, put dark on light or light on dark.

== SMOKE BY DAY, FLAME BY NIGHT ==

- Day: smoke shows farther than flame. Get a hot fire going first, then add green boughs, leaves, or damp punky wood for thick white smoke. Over snow or under overcast, small amounts of pitchy wood make darker smoke that shows better. Add smoke material gradually; never smother the fire.
- Night: flame shows farther than smoke. Keep a bright fire ready to build up fast if you hear aircraft.
- Keep a ready-to-light signal fire laid near camp, protected from rain, so it needs only one flame when the moment comes.

== MOVEMENT, CONTRAST, COLOR ==

A person is hard to see from the air. Improve your odds:

- Get to open ground: meadows, gravel bars, lake shores, burns, or ridgetops if weather allows.
- Spread bright gear, orange, blue, anything unnatural, in geometric shapes. Nature rarely makes straight lines.
- When aircraft approach, move. Wave a bright item over your head in wide, slow arcs. On snow, lie down and sweep your arms like a snow angel. Do not stand still in shadow.

== WHEN HELICOPTERS CAN AND CANNOT SEE YOU ==

- Crews can spot movement, mirror flashes, smoke, strong color, and geometry in the open, even from far away.
- They usually cannot see you under tree canopy, in shadowed drainages, against colors that match your clothing, or at night without a light or fire.
- They cannot land everywhere. If a helicopter sees you, stay put. Secure loose gear, protect your eyes from rotor wash debris, and follow the crew's instructions. Never approach a helicopter until the crew signals you in.
