Reading Tracks and Sign

== WHY READ SIGN ==
- Animals leave a record: what lives here, roughly how many, how recently, and often where the water is.
- You rarely see the animal. You constantly see its sign. Sign tells you what to prepare for and where not to camp.

== PRINTS: THE QUICK READ ==
- Dogs, coyotes, foxes, wolves: oval print, claw marks usually show, symmetrical, with an X of space between the toes and heel pad.
- Cats (bobcat, cougar): round print, no claw marks, one toe leads so the print looks lopsided; the space forms a C, not an X.
- Deer, elk, moose: two-toed heart shape, the point of the heart facing the direction of travel.
- Bear, raccoon, opossum, skunk: five toes. Raccoon and opossum look like little hands; a bear hind print looks like a flat human foot with claws.
- For detailed print identification and sizes, see the Nature section tracks article.

== SCAT ==
- Scat shows diet and species. Tubular droppings full of fur, bone, or hair mean a meat eater (coyote, fox, cat).
- Round pellets in small piles mean a plant eater: deer and rabbit are the common ones.
- Fresh scat is moist and dark; old scat is dry, faded, and crumbly. Wet scat means the animal is close and recent, so give predators room and do not track them.
- Never handle scat with bare hands; it carries parasites and disease.

== TRAILS AND RUNS ==
- Game trails are worn paths animals reuse. They braid together and widen where they lead somewhere useful.
- Where several trails join and head downhill, they often lead to water. In dry country, follow the heavier, more braided trail down.
- Small tunnels worn through grass and brush are runs made by rabbits and rodents.

== FEEDING SIGN ==
- Twigs torn off ragged mean deer, which have no upper front teeth; clean angled cuts mean rabbits or rodents.
- Stripped bark, gnawed cones, and piles of stripped cone scales (middens) mean squirrels or porcupines.
- Turned-over rocks and torn-apart logs can mean a bear foraging. Rooted, dug-up ground can be a bear or a digging animal.
- Fresh feeding sign plus fresh scat plus tracks together mean the animal is using this spot right now.

== BEDS, DENS, AND RUBS ==
- Flattened matted grass is a bed where an animal rested; deer beds cluster in sheltered spots.
- Holes with packed, worn entrances and tracks are active dens. Do not reach into any hole or den.
- Rubbed and gouged tree trunks can be deer working antlers or a bear marking. Claw marks high on a trunk mean bear; note it and camp elsewhere.

== SEASON ==
- Tracks last longest in mud, snow, dust, and wet sand. After rain or fresh snow, everything you see was made since; that is your clock.
- Heavy rubs and torn ground peak in the fall rut, when bucks and bull elk are least predictable.

== AGING A TRACK ==
- Fresh: sharp crisp edges, moist and dark, fine detail intact, nothing blown into it.
- Old: edges crumbled and rounded, floor dried and pale, leaves, dust, or insect tracks inside.
