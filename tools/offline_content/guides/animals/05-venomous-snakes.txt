Venomous Snakes and Their Safe Lookalikes

== THE SAFE DEFAULT ==
- If you are not certain a snake is harmless, treat it as venomous and leave it alone. You never need to touch, kill, or crowd a snake to stay safe.
- No single mark separates venomous from harmless at a safe distance. The rules below help you lean safe, not to justify getting closer.
- Most bites happen to people handling, killing, or photographing snakes up close. Give any snake room and it almost always leaves on its own.
- Do not handle a dead snake or a severed head. Both bite by reflex for up to an hour.

== NORTH AMERICA'S FOUR TO KNOW ==
- Pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperhead, cottonmouth) cause nearly all US venomous bites. They share a heavy body, a broad head clearly wider than the neck, and vertical cat-like pupils. The pupils and heat pits are too close to check safely.
- Rattlesnakes: found in nearly every region. Heavy-bodied, blotched or diamond-patterned, with a rattle of dry segments on the tail. Young have only a button and rattles break off, so no rattle does not mean safe.
- Copperhead: eastern and central woodlands. Copper-tan with hourglass crossbands, narrow across the spine and wide down the sides. Superbly camouflaged in leaf litter, it freezes rather than flees, so most bites come from a hand or foot set too close.
- Cottonmouth, also called water moccasin: Southeast wetlands and slow water. Thick, dark, blocky-headed; gapes a white cottony mouth as a warning and may hold its ground. Swims with the body riding high on the surface.
- Coral snake: Southeast and the desert Southwest, reclusive and rarely seen. Small blunt black snout, with red, yellow, and black rings that fully circle the body and red bands touching yellow bands.

== THE SAFE LOOKALIKES ==
- Rat snakes and gopher or bull snakes are the classic rattlesnake false alarms. Slender-headed, often climbing, they will flatten, hiss, and buzz the tail in dry leaves to bluff. Harmless.
- Water snakes are constantly killed as cottonmouths. They are thinner-necked, sit low in the water, and flee fast. Harmless, though they bite if grabbed. In doubt near water, just give it room.
- Milk snakes, scarlet kingsnakes, and scarlet snakes mimic the coral snake with red, black, and yellow rings. On US native species the band order differs: on a coral snake red touches yellow; on these harmless mimics red touches black.

== THE CORAL SNAKE RHYME AND WHY IT IS NOT ENOUGH ==
- US Southeast only: "red touch yellow, kill a fellow; red touch black, friend of Jack." It is a rough aid, nothing more.
- In Arizona and the desert Southwest the rhyme gets shaky: the harmless shovel-nosed snake can show red touching yellow, and odd coral snake patterns occur. South of the border the rhyme fails outright.
- The rule that never fails: never pick up any red, yellow, and black banded snake, anywhere. Then the rhyme's mistakes cannot hurt you.

== PREVENTION ==
- Watch where you put hands and feet; that one habit prevents most bites. Never reach into rock cracks, brush piles, or holes you cannot see into.
- Step onto logs and rocks and look over the far side before stepping down, never blindly over them.
- Wear boots and long pants in snake country; use a headlamp on warm nights.
- If you hear a rattle, freeze, find the snake by sight, then back away slowly. A snake can strike about half its body length.

== SEASON ==
- Snakes are most active spring through fall. Cold-blooded, they bask on warm mornings and lie on cool rocks or roads on warm evenings, and hunt after dark in hot weather.
- They are sluggish and hidden in cold, but a warm winter day can bring one out. Never assume it is too cold for snakes.

== IF BITTEN ==
- Move away from the snake, keep the victim calm and still, remove rings and tight items, keep the limb near heart level, and evacuate to a hospital. No cutting, sucking, tourniquets, or ice. Full steps are in the first aid bites and stings article.
