OTHER CONTACT HAZARDS

Some plants injure through stings, burning sap, or embedded
spines. Most of these you can treat yourself with what you
carry or improvise, and this article leads with that field
care. Reaching help is one option, not the only answer. For
eye burns and severe allergy it also states plainly what the
field cannot fix, so you can act on the truth.

== STINGING NETTLE ==
- ID: upright plant 2 to 6 feet tall; opposite, toothed, heart-
  shaped to lance-shaped leaves; square stem; tiny hairs on stems
  and leaf undersides; small greenish flower clusters.
- Habitat: moist soil, stream banks, disturbed ground, shade.
- What happens: the hairs inject a mix of irritants (histamine,
  formic acid, and others) causing burning, redness, and raised
  bumps that usually fade within hours to a day.
- FIELD TREATMENT (handle this yourself):
  - Do not rub or scratch. Rubbing drives hairs deeper and
    spreads the fluid.
  - Do not touch the spot with the plant-contaminated hand.
  - Lift the embedded hairs first: press tape onto the skin and
    peel it off, repeating with fresh tape. A thin layer of dried
    mud or peeled-off glue also lifts hairs.
  - Rinse with cool water. A cool wet cloth or cold stream water
    eases the burning.
  - A baking soda paste calms the acid part of the sting. If you
    carry antihistamine tablets or cream, they cut the itch.
  - Crushed dock or jewelweed leaf rubbed on is a long-used folk
    remedy. It is unproven, but low risk; the cool moisture may be
    what actually helps.
- Most stings need nothing more and settle on their own.
- RARE but serious: a few people react with spreading hives,
  swelling of the lips or throat, wheezing, or faintness. That is
  an allergic reaction. If you carry an epinephrine auto-injector,
  use it in the outer thigh. There is no reliable field substitute
  for epinephrine: keep the person calm, sitting up if breathing is
  hard or lying flat with legs raised if faint, and rest them.
  Evacuate if you can reach help.
- Edible when cooked: heat destroys the sting. Never eat it raw,
  and be fully certain of ID first. See the foraging notes.

== WILD PARSNIP AND GIANT HOGWEED ==
- What they do: sap on skin plus sunlight causes a chemical burn
  (phytophotodermatitis). Chemicals in the sap (furanocoumarins)
  react with UV light. Redness and deep blisters can appear hours
  later, often the next day.
- Wild parsnip: 2 to 5 feet tall; flat-topped clusters of small
  YELLOW flowers; grooved stem; leaves with toothed leaflets.
- Giant hogweed: very large, 8 to 14 feet tall; huge umbrella-
  shaped clusters of white flowers; thick green stem with purple
  blotches and coarse hairs; leaves up to 5 feet wide. Rare but
  serious; its burns can be deep and scarring.
- FIRST MINUTES (do at once, this is what limits the damage):
  - Get the skin OUT of sunlight immediately. Light drives the
    burn, so shade or cover the area first.
  - Wash the sap off fast with soap and water. The sooner the
    better, because the chemical soaks in within minutes. Rinse
    well, then wash again.
  - Keep the skin covered and out of the sun for at least 48
    hours. Hogweed can leave skin sun-sensitive for weeks to
    years, so keep healing skin shaded or covered long after.
- IF BLISTERS OR BURNS FORM (manage it in the field):
  - Do NOT pop blisters. Intact skin is your best natural bandage
    against infection. Leave them alone.
  - If a blister tears open on its own, trim only the loose dead
    skin, rinse with the cleanest water you have (boiled and cooled
    is best), and cover it.
  - Cover burns with the cleanest cloth or dressing you have,
    loosely, not tight.
  - Change the dressing twice a day and any time it gets wet or
    dirty.
  - If help is days away, a thin layer of honey or antibiotic
    ointment under the dressing lowers the infection risk on open
    burns.
  - Cool wet compresses ease pain. Ibuprofen or acetaminophen if
    carried helps too.
  - Drink water. Burns leak fluid and dehydrate you.
  - Watch for infection: spreading redness, red streaks up the
    limb, pus, a bad smell, or fever. These mean the wound is
    turning dangerous.
- SAP IN THE EYES:
  - Flush the eye with clean water for several minutes, right away,
    holding the lid open.
  - Shield the eye from light: keep it closed, or cover with a
    clean cloth or pad.
  - Hard truth: hogweed sap in the eye can cause lasting or
    permanent vision loss, and nothing in the field reverses it.
    Immediate flushing and keeping the eye out of light give the
    best odds. Protect the person and guide their footing while
    their vision is impaired.
- Evacuate if you can safely reach help, above all for eye contact,
  burns over a large area, or spreading infection. If you cannot,
  the wound care above IS your plan: keep it clean, covered, out of
  sun, and watched.

== CACTUS AND FINE SPINES ==
- Large spines: grip close to the skin and pull straight out with
  tweezers, pliers, or a multitool. Pulling at an angle snaps them
  off under the skin.
- Fine hair-like spines (glochids, as on prickly pear) lodge by the
  dozen and break off. They are the real problem.
- Best removal order, which beats any single method:
  1. Lift the big clumps first with tweezers or a fine comb.
  2. Then spread a thin layer of white glue over the area, press a
     piece of gauze or thin cloth into it, let it dry about 30
     minutes, and peel it off. The fine spines come out with the
     glue and cloth.
  - Glue plus cloth removes far more than tape. Use tape only if
    you have nothing else, pressing and peeling with fresh strips.
- After removal: wash with clean water, pat dry, keep it clean.
- Do not squeeze the skin to force spines out. That drives them
  deeper and invites infection.
- Spines too deep to reach: leave them rather than dig and tear the
  skin. Keep the area clean and watch it. A retained spine may form
  a sore lump over days; warm soaks a few times a day can bring a
  shallow one to the surface where you can lift it out. Watch for
  spreading redness or pus.

== SEASON ==
- These are warm-season hazards, worst spring through fall when
  the plants are green and growing.
- Wild parsnip and giant hogweed burns peak in summer: strong
  sunlight plus fresh sap drives the reaction.
- Stinging nettle stings hardest on lush spring and summer
  growth. Prickly pear glochids are a year-round risk.

== PREVENTION AND GENERAL ==
- When unsure, do not touch.
- Long sleeves, long pants, and gloves prevent most contact
  injuries on the trail.
- After handling any suspect plant, wash your hands before touching
  your face or eyes.
- Rinse tools, gloves, and clothing that touched sap. Dried parsnip
  and hogweed sap stays active and can burn you later.
