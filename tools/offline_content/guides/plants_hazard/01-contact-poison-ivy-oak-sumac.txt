POISON IVY, OAK, AND SUMAC

These three plants carry urushiol, an oil that causes an itchy,
blistering rash in most people. Contact is enough; you do not
need to eat them. The rash is miserable but almost never
deadly, and it clears on its own in 1 to 3 weeks. Your job in
the field is simple: get the oil off fast, calm the itch, and
keep the skin from getting infected. You can do all of that
yourself with what you carry or can improvise.

== FIRST: IF YOU JUST TOUCHED IT, WASH NOW ==
This is the single most useful thing you can do, and it is
100 percent do-it-yourself. Urushiol starts binding to skin
within minutes and is locked in within a few hours. Washing
within about 10 minutes stops the rash in many people. Even an
hour or more later it is still worth washing to remove oil you
can still get off and to stop spreading it to new skin.
- Use LOTS of cool or lukewarm running water. Volume matters
  more than soap. Rinse under a flow; do not soak in one small
  pool that just redeposits the oil.
- Add soap if you have it, especially a grease-cutting dish
  soap. Urushiol is an oil. Rinse well and often.
- Rubbing alcohol or an alcohol wipe dissolves the oil: wipe,
  then rinse with water. Alcohol hand sanitizer works too.
- Scrub gently with fingertips under flowing water, NOT a
  washcloth or sponge that just smears oil around.
- Clean under your fingernails. That is where hidden oil rides.
- Cool water, not hot, while the oil is still on you. Hot water
  helps it spread and soak in.
- Branded products (Tecnu, Zanfel) work but are only slightly
  better than plain soap and water. Do not wait for them.
- No clean water at all? Crushed jewelweed stems rubbed on and
  rinsed can help a little, but ONLY because they act like a
  weak soap. Plain water or real soap is as good or better.
  Do not trust any plant as a cure.

== THEN DECONTAMINATE EVERYTHING ==
The oil rides on gear and keeps re-dosing you for a long time.
- Urushiol stays active on clothing, tools, boots, and pack
  straps for a year or more, sometimes several years, if not
  washed off.
- Handle contaminated gear with gloves or a barrier such as a
  plastic bag over your hand. Wash it with soap and hot water,
  separately from clean items.
- Wipe hard gear and tool handles with alcohol.
- Rinse the dog (gloves on) before it spreads oil to everyone
  who pets it.

== IF THE RASH HAS ALREADY STARTED: FIELD CARE ==
The rash shows up 12 to 72 hours after exposure: redness,
swelling, blisters, hard itch, often in streaks where the
plant brushed you. It is self-limiting. Nothing in a pack
makes it vanish overnight, but you can control it and keep it
from getting worse. Work down these options by what you have.

Option 1, best kit:
- Apply a topical steroid cream EARLY: over-the-counter
  hydrocortisone 1 percent, or a stronger prescription steroid
  if you carry one. It works best in the first days and on
  intact (not broken-open) skin.
- Take an ORAL antihistamine at night. A sedating one such as
  diphenhydramine helps you sleep through the itch.
- Cool compresses on the worst spots as needed.

Option 2, basic supplies:
- Cool wet compresses or short cool soaks several times a day.
  They knock down itch and dry weeping blisters.
- Calamine lotion, or aluminum acetate (Burow's solution /
  Domeboro) as an astringent, to dry and soothe.
- Colloidal oatmeal soak: grind plain oats to a fine powder,
  tie it in cloth, and soak the skin in cool water. Oat starch
  calms the itch.
- Baking soda: a paste (baking soda plus a little water) on
  hot spots, or a spoonful stirred into a cool soak.

Option 3, nothing but water:
- Repeated cool water soaks and air-drying are the workhorse.
  They genuinely reduce itch and dry the blisters.
- A cool saltwater (saline) compress acts as a mild drying
  astringent for weeping areas.
- Keep the area clean and open to air when you can.
- Keep nails short and do NOT scratch. Distract yourself and
  ride it out. It clears in 1 to 3 weeks on its own.

Blisters:
- The clear fluid does NOT contain urushiol and does NOT spread
  the rash. New patches come from more oil, not from the fluid.
- Leave blisters intact. Unbroken skin is your infection
  barrier. If a big blister tears open, keep it clean and cover
  it with a clean dry dressing.

== IF YOU CARRY ORAL STEROIDS (SEVERE, WIDESPREAD RASH) ==
For a severe or widespread reaction, oral steroids are the one
thing that truly blunts it, if you carry them and know the dose.
- Typical course: prednisone about 1 mg per kg of body weight
  per day, tapered slowly over 2 to 3 WEEKS.
- Do NOT use a short 5 to 7 day course or a 6-day dose pack for
  this. Too short, and the rash rebounds hard when you stop.
- If you are unsure of the dose, or you have diabetes or other
  medical conditions, this is a real reason to evacuate if you
  can safely reach help.

== DANGER SIGNS AND THE HARD TRUTH ==
You handle most cases yourself. These few are serious. Field-
manage them, and also evacuate if you can safely reach help:
- Rash on the face near the eyes, an eye swelling shut, or the
  genitals involved.
- Rash covering a large part of the body.
- You breathed smoke from burning plants, or your mouth or
  throat was exposed, AND you have trouble breathing or
  swallowing.
Honest truth: airway swelling and a true whole-body allergic
reaction (anaphylaxis) can kill, and there is NO field
substitute for epinephrine and advanced care. If you carry an
epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis, this is what it is
for; use it and get help. Without it, keep the airway as open
as you can (sit upright, stay calm, breathe cool air) and move
toward help. Do not pretend a plant, a cream, or a home trick
fixes a closing airway. It does not.
For the eyes: flush with plenty of clean water. The lids can
swell dramatically; the rash itself still resolves, but protect
your vision and evacuate if you cannot see.

== INFECTION WATCH ==
Over days, infection of scratched-open skin, not the rash
itself, is the real field danger.
- Warning signs: spreading redness and warmth, pus, red streaks
  running up a limb, fever, or pain that grows AFTER the rash
  had started improving.
- Prevent it: keep skin clean, keep nails short, cover open
  areas with a clean dry dressing.
- If you carry oral antibiotics and see clear signs of skin
  infection, that is when they earn their place. Evacuate if
  the infection keeps spreading and you cannot control it.

== WHAT NOT TO DO ==
- Do NOT use topical antihistamine creams (diphenhydramine
  cream) or topical anesthetics (benzocaine and other "-caine"
  products). They can make you MORE sensitized and worsen the
  reaction. Oral antihistamines are fine; the creams are not.
- Do NOT burn these plants. Smoke carries urushiol into the
  eyes, throat, and lungs and can cause a dangerous internal
  reaction. This is the one exposure that can be deadly.
- Do NOT scratch, and do NOT deliberately pop blisters.
- Do NOT scrub raw skin with harsh solvents like gasoline or
  bleach. You would trade a rash for a chemical burn.

== IDENTIFY AND AVOID (YOUR BEST DEFENSE) ==
Not touching it beats every treatment. Learn the plants.
- "Leaves of three, let it be" is a useful start but NOT
  complete. Poison ivy and oak usually show three leaflets, but
  so do many harmless plants, so treat any three-leaflet plant
  as suspect, not as proof. Poison sumac breaks this rule.
- Poison ivy: three pointed leaflets, middle one on a longer
  stalk; edges smooth, toothed, or lobed; grows as a vine or
  low shrub; often a hairy "furry rope" vine on trees; found
  across most of North America.
- Poison oak: three leaflets with rounded, lobed, oak-like
  edges; shrub or vine; more common in the West and Southeast.
- Poison sumac: 7 to 13 leaflets in pairs along a reddish stem;
  a shrub or small tree of wet swamps and bogs in the East.
- All three can show clusters of small white or cream berries.
  White berries on a woody plant are a strong warning sign.
- Black-dot sign: urushiol turns black in air, so small black
  dots or streaks can mark these plants. Observe only. Never
  handle a suspect plant to check.

== THE OIL IS ACTIVE YEAR-ROUND ==
- Bare winter stems and leafless vines still carry urushiol.
  Do not grab an unknown hairy vine for a handhold in any
  season.
- Spring and summer bring the most exposure from green leaves.
- In fall the leaflets often turn bright red or orange. Do not
  reach for the colorful foliage.
- If you know you are heading into it: a barrier product with
  bentoquatam (organoclay, sold as IvyBlock) applied 15 minutes
  before exposure and reapplied every 4 hours reduces the
  reaction. Long sleeves, long pants, and gloves help even more.
