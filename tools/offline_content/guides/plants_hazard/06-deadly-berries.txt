DEADLY AND TOXIC BERRIES

Most wild-berry poisonings cause vomiting and
diarrhea. The real killer from those is dehydration,
and you CAN fight that in the field. A few berries
(yew seed, moonseed, baneberry) attack the heart or
nerves and can kill with no field cure. Know which
one you face. Color is not a safe guide. When in
doubt, do not eat it.

== IF SOMEONE ATE A TOXIC BERRY: FIRST STEPS ==
- Clear the mouth. Have them spit out any plant.
  Rinse the mouth with clean water.
- Do NOT force vomiting. It removes little toxin and
  vomit can choke a drowsy person. The old "make them
  throw up" advice is wrong and dropped by doctors.
- Save a sample: one berry, a leaf, a bit of stem,
  and any vomit. Note the time eaten and how many.
- Sit them down, keep them calm and warm, and watch
  closely. Symptoms can take up to 6 hours to start.

== CONTROL SYMPTOMS YOURSELF ==
Vomiting and diarrhea (the common case):
- Let mild vomiting happen; do not fight it. The
  threat is fluid loss, not the vomiting itself.
- Replace fluids in small, frequent sips. Gulping
  restarts vomiting.
- Field rehydration drink: 1 quart (about 1 liter)
  clean water, 6 level teaspoons sugar, half a level
  teaspoon salt. Mix well and sip all day. Too much
  salt is harmful, so measure it. Use packaged oral
  rehydration salts instead if you carry them.

Mouth or throat burning (Virginia creeper and others):
- Rinse the mouth. Sip cool water, or milk if you
  have it. Cold, smooth food soothes it.
- Watch for swelling that blocks the throat. Trouble
  breathing or swallowing is the real danger.

Activated charcoal (only if you actually carry it):
- Only real pharmacy activated charcoal binds toxins.
  Do NOT use campfire charcoal. It does not work and
  may add harm. This is not a folk substitute.
- Adult dose about 50 grams (range 25 to 100 g) stirred
  into water. Works best within 1 hour of eating.
- Give ONLY if the person is fully awake, can swallow,
  and is not vomiting hard. NEVER give it to anyone
  drowsy, seizing, or unable to protect their airway.
  They can inhale it into the lungs.

== IF THEY GET WORSE AND YOU CANNOT REACH CARE ==
Drowsy or passing out:
- Roll them onto their side (recovery position) so the
  airway stays open and vomit drains out.
- Give NOTHING by mouth: no water, food, or charcoal.
- Keep them warm. Check breathing often.

Seizures:
- Move hard or sharp objects away. Cushion the head.
- Do NOT hold them down or put anything in the mouth.
- Time it. When it stops, roll them onto their side.

Heart and nerve toxins (yew seed, moonseed, baneberry):
- Hard truth: taxine (yew) and dauricine (moonseed)
  poison the heart rhythm and nerves. There is NO
  field antidote and no folk cure. Even hospitals can
  only support with pacing and machines.
- What you CAN do: total rest. Exertion strains a
  failing heart. Do not let them walk or work. Keep
  them lying down, calm, and warm. Small sips of water
  only if fully awake. Stay with them.
- If the heart stops and you are trained, start CPR.
  In the backcountry with no defibrillator the odds
  are very low, but CPR is the only option left.
- Be honest with them and yourself about the odds.

== EVACUATE IF YOU CAN SAFELY REACH HELP ==
- This is ONE option among the steps above, not the
  first and only answer. Do the field care first; it
  stands on its own.
- If help is reachable without creating new danger,
  send word or move the patient. Bring the plant
  sample and your notes on time, amount, and symptoms.
- The same dose hits children and small adults hardest.
  Prioritize them.

== POKEWEED ==
- Tall, up to 10 feet, smooth reddish-purple stems.
- Dark purple berries hang in long drooping clusters.
- Roots, stems, and leaves are the most toxic; raw
  berries less so but still cause severe vomiting,
  cramps, and diarrhea. Onset up to 6 hours.
- Usually survivable with rest and fluids. Do not eat.
  Young greens are eaten only after several changes of
  boiling water; not a field food.

== NIGHTSHADES ==
- Bittersweet: vine, purple star-shaped flowers with
  yellow centers; berries turn green to bright red in
  clusters.
- Black nightshade: small white star flowers; small
  round black berries.
- Toxin is solanine: burning mouth, vomiting, cramps,
  diarrhea. Usually GI and survivable; large amounts
  can bring drowsiness and confusion.
- Ripe black-nightshade berries are eaten cooked in
  some places, but green berries and leaves are toxic
  and look-alikes are risky. Treat all wild ones as
  unsafe.

== BANEBERRY (DOLL'S-EYES) ==
- White baneberry: shiny white berries, each with a
  dark spot, on thick red stalks. Red baneberry has
  red berries.
- Causes burning mouth and throat, severe cramps,
  vomiting, and bloody diarrhea, and may affect the
  heart. Treat as serious, above all in children.
- A few berries can badly sicken a child. Do not eat.

== MOONSEED (GRAPE LOOK-ALIKE) ==
- Toxic vine that mimics wild grape and has killed
  foragers, including children.
- Check the SEED: moonseed has ONE flat crescent
  (moon-shaped) seed; true grapes have one to a few
  round, pear-shaped seeds.
- Moonseed lacks grape tendrils and has smooth (not
  toothed) leaf edges. Do not judge by leaf alone.
- Toxin dauricine can cause seizures and dangerous
  heart rhythms, with no field cure. Never taste to
  decide. Split a berry and check the seed. Crescent,
  or unsure, do not eat.

== VIRGINIA CREEPER ==
- Climbing vine with FIVE leaflets per stem (poison
  ivy has three) and dark blue-black berries on red
  stalks.
- Berries carry oxalate crystals: burning mouth and
  throat, drooling, vomiting, and stomach pain. Large
  amounts can harm the kidneys. Do not eat them.

== HOLLY AND YEW ==
- Holly: spiny evergreen leaves and bright red berries.
  Several berries cause vomiting and diarrhea; the main
  risk is dehydration. Rarely dangerous to adults, but
  keep the berries away from children.
- Yew: evergreen with flat needles and a red berry-like
  aril. The soft red flesh is the least harmful part.
  The hard SEED, plus the needles and bark, carry
  taxine and can stop the heart. Onset 30 to 60 minutes.
- Hard truth on yew: a real dose has no field antidote,
  and standard heart drugs fail even in hospitals.
  Field care is rest, calm, and supportive only. Never
  eat any part of a yew, and keep children away.

== RULE ==
- No wild berry enters the mouth without certain ID.
- Swallowing the seed is what makes yew and moonseed
  deadly. Spit seeds out and rinse if any got in.
- Teach children never to eat wild berries.
