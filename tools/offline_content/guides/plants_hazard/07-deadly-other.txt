OTHER DEADLY PLANTS

These plants poison if eaten. Several grow around homes,
gardens, and disturbed ground. Treat all parts as unsafe and
keep children and pets away. This guide leads with what to do
if someone has ALREADY eaten one and you cannot reach a
doctor. Plant IDs are further down. If unsure of a plant, do
not eat it.

== IF SOMEONE HAS EATEN ONE: DO THIS FIRST ==
- Stay calm. Stop the exposure. Have them spit out any plant
  still in the mouth and wipe the mouth out with a finger or
  cloth.
- Do NOT make them vomit (see the DO NOT list).
- Activated charcoal, IF you carry it: give it only while the
  person is fully awake and able to swallow, and best within
  about 1 hour of eating. Adult dose about 50 g (1 g per kg),
  stirred into water. It binds jimsonweed, foxglove, oleander,
  ricin, and buckeye toxins in the gut. Do not force it. Skip
  it if they are drowsy, confused, or seizing (choking risk).
- Rest, lying down or on the side. No walking, no exertion.
  Effort strains a poisoned heart and drives the toxin around
  the body faster. This matters most for oleander and foxglove.
- Sip fluids if fully awake: water, or a rehydration drink
  (1 liter water, a pinch of salt, a small scoop of sugar).
  This replaces fluid lost to vomiting and diarrhea.
- If drowsy or vomiting, roll them onto their side (recovery
  position) so they cannot choke. Never pour liquid into a
  sleepy or limp person.
- Note the plant, the part, how much, and the time eaten. Keep
  a piece of the plant if you safely can.
- Watch for danger signs: fainting, a very slow or very fast
  pulse, seizures, trouble breathing, no urine passed.
- Evacuate ONLY if you can reach help without the patient
  having to exert themselves. If you cannot, the steps above
  ARE the treatment. Carry them or bring help to them; do not
  make a poisoned person walk out.

== DO NOT ==
- Do NOT induce vomiting. No salt water, no finger down the
  throat, no ipecac. Vomiting rarely clears enough toxin and
  can choke a drowsy or seizing person (common with jimsonweed)
  or burn the throat a second time.
- Do NOT give charcoal to anyone drowsy, confused, or seizing.
- Do NOT let a heart-poisoned person exert themselves.
- Do NOT trust folk cures, drawing poultices, or drinking oil
  or milk to neutralize the poison. They do not work and some
  make it worse.

== THE HONEST LIMITS ==
Some of these poisons have no field cure, and no real cure even
in a hospital without drugs and monitors you will not have:
- Castor bean (ricin): no antidote anywhere.
- Oleander and foxglove: the fix is a hospital antidote (digoxin
  antibody) plus heart monitoring.
- Severe jimsonweed: needs hospital sedation and cooling.
For all of these, early charcoal, rest, fluids, cooling, and
airway protection are the best you can do, and they DO save
lives in milder poisonings. A large dose may be fatal whatever
you do. Do the steps anyway. Do not waste effort on fake cures.

== JIMSONWEED / DATURA ==
- Coarse plant, large white to purple trumpet flowers, spiny
  seed pods full of dark seeds; foul-smelling leaves.
- Disturbed soil, fields, roadsides across North America.
- Seeds and all parts cause anticholinergic poisoning: dry
  flushed skin, wide pupils, blurred vision, racing heart, high
  fever, agitation, hallucinations, delirium; can kill. Effects
  can last 1 to 3 days because it slows the gut.
- If eaten: give charcoal early, but only while still awake.
  After that the job is to keep them COOL and SAFE. Move to
  shade, strip off layers, wet the skin and fan it to fight the
  fever, and dim bright light for their eyes. Stay with them and
  gently keep them from wandering or hurting themselves. Do not
  fight or tightly restrain them; struggling raises body heat.
  Sip water. Most people who are kept cool and safe ride it out
  over 1 to 2 days.

== WHITE SNAKEROOT ==
- Woodland plant, 1 to 4 ft, clusters of small fluffy white
  flowers, toothed pointed leaves set opposite each other.
- Poison is tremetol. It passes into the milk and meat of
  animals that graze it; people are usually poisoned that way
  ("milk sickness"), which killed many settlers. Eating the
  plant directly is also toxic.
- Signs: weakness, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, tremors,
  belly pain.
- If eaten or suspected: stop eating the source at once. Rest
  and sip rehydration fluid steadily; the illness is drawn out.
  Supportive care only. There is no antidote.

== CASTOR BEAN ==
- Large ornamental, star-shaped leaves, spiny pods holding
  mottled bean-like seeds. Escapes gardens in warm areas.
- Seeds contain ricin, among the deadliest plant toxins. A few
  thoroughly chewed seeds can kill. Swallowed whole and unbroken
  they are far less dangerous, because the hard shell blocks the
  toxin.
- Signs in 4 to 10 hours: burning mouth, vomiting, heavy and
  sometimes bloody diarrhea, then collapse.
- If chewed or eaten: give charcoal early if carried. There is
  NO antidote. Your one useful field action is to replace fluids
  without stopping: rehydration drink in small, frequent sips,
  day and night, because death comes largely from fluid loss and
  shock. Rest, keep warm. A large dose may still be fatal; give
  the fluids anyway.

== FOXGLOVE ==
- Tall spike of tubular, thimble-shaped flowers, pink, purple,
  or white with spotted throats; soft fuzzy leaves.
- Leaves are sometimes mistaken for comfrey or other edible
  greens. Do not eat them.
- Contains cardiac glycosides. Causes nausea, vomiting, vision
  changes (yellow or green haze), and dangerous slow or
  irregular heartbeats.
- If eaten: give charcoal early if carried. Then absolute rest,
  lying down, NO exertion, for a full day; effort can trigger a
  fatal heart rhythm. Sip water. The real fix is a hospital
  antidote you will not have, so keep them still and calm and
  check the pulse by hand.

== OLEANDER ==
- Evergreen shrub, narrow leathery leaves, clusters of pink,
  white, or red flowers; common in warm southern ranges.
- All parts are extremely toxic and act on the heart, like
  foxglove. Do not eat any part, do not use stems as skewers, do
  not drink water or tea it has steeped in, and do not cook over
  or breathe its burning smoke.
- If eaten: treat exactly like foxglove. Charcoal early if
  carried, then absolute rest and no exertion, sip water, watch
  the pulse. No field antidote; a large dose can be fatal.

== HORSE CHESTNUT vs EDIBLE CHESTNUT ==
- Horse chestnut (buckeye) is TOXIC (esculin); true sweet
  chestnut is edible.
- Husk: horse chestnut has a thick husk with few, thick, widely
  spaced spines; true chestnut has a dense husk covered in many
  fine, sharp spines, like a hedgehog.
- Nuts: horse chestnut usually holds one smooth, rounded nut;
  true chestnut holds two to three flatter nuts with a
  tassel-like tip.
- Leaves: horse chestnut has leaflets spread like fingers from
  one point; true chestnut has single long, toothed leaves.
- If eaten: signs come in 15 minutes to 3 hours: belly pain,
  vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, poor coordination. Give charcoal
  early if carried, rest, and sip rehydration fluid. Most people
  recover over 2 to 3 days with fluids and rest. If unsure which
  chestnut it is, do not eat it.
