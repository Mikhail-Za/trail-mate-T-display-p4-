Improvised Cordage and Gear

== THE MINDSET ==
- Improvisation buys time; it does not replace packing right. Think in functions: what does this job need - tension, insulation, waterproofing, closure?
- Test every improvised item gently before betting anything important on it.

== CORDAGE FROM PLANTS: THE REVERSE WRAP ==
- Good fibers: inner bark from dead standing willow, basswood, or cedar; dry tough grasses; the outer fibers of nettle and dogbane stalks (crush the stalk, split it, peel the fibers free). Long, flexible, and stringy is what you want.
- Reverse wrap basics: take a thin bundle of fibers and twist it in one direction until it kinks in the middle. Fold at the kink so you hold two strands side by side.
- Twist the strand farther from you AWAY from you, then cross it TOWARD you over the other strand. Repeat: twist away, cross toward. The two strands lock against each other and cannot unravel.
- To lengthen, lay new fibers alongside a strand a few inches before it runs out and keep wrapping across the splice.
- Two-strand plant cord is strong enough for lashing and shelter work. Never trust it for climbing, rappelling, or river work.

== CORDAGE FROM CLOTHING AND GEAR ==
- Shoelaces, drawstrings, pack straps, and jacket hem cords are cordage you are already carrying.
- A T-shirt cut in a spiral makes one long strong strip; twist or braid strips for more strength.
- Paracord hides seven inner strands: pull them out for sewing thread, fishing line, and fine lashings.

== DUCT TAPE ==
- Wrap several feet around a water bottle or trekking pole so it is always with you.
- Uses: covering hot spots and blisters (over a clean dressing), patching jackets, tents, and sleeping pads, wrapping a cracked pole or knife handle, strapping a flapping boot sole, padding and securing a splint, twisting a long strip into emergency cord, covering sharp edges.

== TRASH BAGS ==
- Two heavy-duty bags weigh almost nothing and answer many problems.
- Rain gear: cut a face-sized hole near one bottom corner and wear the bag as a hooded poncho. Keep the hole small; heat escapes at the neck.
- Water carry: line a pack, hat, or stuff sack with a bag and tie the neck.
- Insulation: stuff a bag with dry leaves or grass for a sitting pad or crude vest; lay a bag between you and wet ground to block conductive heat loss.
- Pack liner: keeps your insulation dry in all-day rain, which matters more than keeping yourself dry.

== SAFETY PINS ==
- Close torn clothing and gear, pin a sling for an injured arm, replace a blown zipper pull, hang gear to dry, and drain a blister after sterilizing the point in a flame.

== EMERGENCY BLANKET, BEYOND WRAPPING ==
- Heat reflector: pitch it behind you, facing a small fire, to bounce warmth onto your back.
- Ground layer under your body; overhead mini-tarp in drizzle; funnel and pooling sheet for collecting rainwater.
- Signal: the mirrored surface flashes sunlight for miles and stands out to searchers; small tied-on strips flutter as markers.

== IMPROVISED SUNGLASSES ==
- Snow blindness is sunburn of the eyes. It can strike on bright snow even under overcast, hurts badly, and can take days to heal.
- Cut narrow horizontal slits in cardboard, bark, cloth, or duct tape folded back on itself, and tie the mask over your eyes.
- Keep the slits thin, a couple of millimeters. Rubbing soot or charcoal under your eyes cuts glare further.
- Better still: never travel on snow without real eye protection.
