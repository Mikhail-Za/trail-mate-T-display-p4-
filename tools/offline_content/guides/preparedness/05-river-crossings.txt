River Crossings

== RESPECT THE HAZARD ==
- Moving water kills experienced hikers. Knee-deep fast current can knock an adult down, and cold water steals strength in minutes.
- Deciding NOT to cross is a skill. A long detour or an overnight wait is always cheaper than a swim.

== WHEN NOT TO CROSS ==
- Above rapids, falls, or strainers. A strainer is a downed tree or brush pile that lets water through but pins a swimmer under. Never cross where being swept means going into one.
- Thigh-deep or deeper AND fast. Depth times speed is the killer, not depth alone.
- Snowmelt rivers rise through the afternoon as the day warms. Cross at the early-morning low, or camp and wait; many rivers drop dramatically overnight.
- Murky floodwater hiding the bottom, floating debris, or ice on the banks.
- Anyone in the group who is frightened, exhausted, or too small for the current.

== SCOUT FIRST ==
- Walk the bank both ways. Look for wide, braided, shallow sections; wider usually means slower and shallower.
- Check the run-out: what happens 100 meters downstream if someone falls? It should be a calm pool or gravel bar, never a rapid or a strainer.
- Straight stretches beat bends. The outside of a bend is deep and fast.
- Toss a stick in to judge speed. Faster than your slow walking pace deserves suspicion.

== SET UP ==
- Unbuckle your hip belt and sternum strap before entering. A buckled pack can pin a swimmer face-down; an unbuckled one slips off and can even float you briefly.
- Keep your shoes on. Bare feet fail on slick rock and cut easily.
- Use a trekking pole or stout stick as a third leg. Loose long clothing that drags current comes off or gets rolled up.

== TECHNIQUE, SOLO ==
- Face UPSTREAM, lean into the current on the pole, and shuffle sideways. Keep two points of contact on the bottom at all times.
- Angle your path diagonally downstream, moving with the push instead of fighting straight across.
- Do not stare at the moving water; it wrecks your balance. Look at your foot placements and the far bank.

== GROUP TECHNIQUES ==
- Tripod: three people face inward in a triangle, grip shoulders or pack straps, and move one person at a time while two brace. Strongest person takes the upstream corner.
- Line astern: form a single-file line facing upstream, strongest person in front breaking the current with a pole, each person gripping the pack or hips of the one ahead. The line shuffles across together.
- Never tie a person to a rope stretched across the current. A fixed rope in moving water pulls swimmers under. Rope work in rivers belongs to trained teams.

== IF YOU ARE SWEPT ==
- Roll onto your back, feet DOWNSTREAM, toes near the surface, arms out to steer. Your feet fend off rocks.
- Do not try to stand in fast current. A foot caught between rocks while current pushes you over is lethal (foot entrapment). Wait for slow, shallow water before standing.
- Backstroke and angle toward the nearest safe bank. Ditch the pack if it drags you down; gear is replaceable.

== AFTER THE CROSSING ==
- Wet plus wind equals hypothermia. Once across, wring clothes, add dry layers, eat something, and keep moving to rebuild warmth.
- Anyone who swam gets treated as cold-stressed: dry clothes, insulation from the ground, warm sweet drinks if available, and an eye kept on them for the next hour.
