Pine Needle Tea (Pinus species): vitamin C

== WHAT IT DOES ==
- A weak tea from pine needles supplies vitamin C. It was used on long winter trips against scurvy.
- It is mild and supportive, not a drug. Keep expectations low.

== DEADLY LOOKALIKE: YEW ==
- The critical danger is YEW (Taxus), which is DEADLY. A small amount can kill an adult.
- TRUE PINES: needles grow in BUNDLES of 2 to 5, each bundle wrapped at its base by a small papery sheath. Woody cones. A strong pine-resin smell.
- YEW: flat, soft needles attached SINGLY in two rows, no papery sheath, no woody cone. It bears a red berry-like cup with a single seed. If the needles are single and flat, DO NOT make tea.
- See the edible Pine entry for the full identification. Learn the yew difference cold before you use this.

== CONIFERS TO AVOID ==
- Do not use yew (deadly), Norfolk Island pine (not a true pine), or any flat-needled evergreen you cannot positively tell apart from yew.
- Ponderosa pine and some relatives are linked to abortion in livestock. Pregnant or nursing women should skip pine needle tea entirely.
- Spruce, fir, and hemlock-tree needles are used by some for tea, but if you cannot rule out yew with total confidence, stick to bundled-needle pines only.

== HOW TO MAKE IT ==
- Chop a small handful of fresh young green needles.
- Steep 5 to 10 minutes in hot water taken OFF the boil. Boiling drives off the vitamin C and brings out a turpentine taste. Strain.
- Keep it weak. A strong turpentine flavor means too many needles or too long a steep.

== CAUTION ==
- Skip it entirely in pregnancy and while nursing.
- Do not drink large amounts. Keep it weak and occasional.
- If you have any doubt about the tree, do not drink it.

== SEASON ==
- Evergreen and available year-round. Young spring needles are the mildest and best.
