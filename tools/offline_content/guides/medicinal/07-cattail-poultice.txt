Cattail Poultice (Typha species): first-aid uses

== WHAT IT DOES ==
- The same cattail from the edible section also has field first-aid uses. Topical.
- The clear, sticky gel between the young inner leaves near the base is soothing and mildly antiseptic. Folk use puts it on small cuts, scrapes, stings, and minor burns.
- A mashed root (rhizome) poultice can be pressed on a minor wound or sting.
- The dry brown seed-head fluff makes soft, absorbent padding over a dressing. It is not sterile, so a clean cloth is better if you have one.

== IDENTIFY: SEE THE EDIBLE ENTRY ==
- Full identification is in the edible Cattail entry. Confirm it there before use.
- Quick check: tall, strap-like leaves rooted in water or muck, and the brown, sausage-shaped seed head.

== LOOKALIKE ==
- IRIS is TOXIC and grows in the same wet ground. Its leaves rise in a FLAT FAN and it never makes a brown sausage head. A flat fan of leaves means walk away. See the edible entry.

== HOW TO USE ==
- Clean the skin first.
- Scoop the sticky gel from between the young leaves, or mash a cleaned rhizome, and apply it to the minor wound. Cover.
- Use the fluff only as padding over a clean dressing, not packed down into a wound.

== CAUTION ==
- Topical first aid only.
- Cattails soak up pollutants from their water. Do not harvest from roadside ditches, farm runoff, or any suspect water, even for skin use.
- Control bleeding and clean the wound first (see the first-aid section).

== SEASON ==
- Gel and green growth come spring into summer. Seed-head fluff is ready late summer through winter.
