Cattail Poultice (Typha species): first-aid uses

== WHAT THIS IS FOR ==
- Scope: MINOR cuts, scrapes, stings, and minor burns. Topical only.
- Cattail is backup dressing MATERIAL when your kit is empty. It is not a
  disinfectant and not a substitute for cleaning the wound.
- The real medicine for a small wound is: stop bleeding, flush it clean,
  keep it covered. Cattail just gives you materials to do that.

== DO THIS FIRST: CLEAN WOUND CARE ==
Works with or without cattail. This is the part that actually prevents
infection.
- Stop bleeding: firm DIRECT pressure with the cleanest cloth you have,
  hold 10 to 15 minutes without peeking. Raise the part above the heart.
- Clean your hands and the skin around the wound as best you can.
- Flush the wound HARD with lots of clean water: drinking-safe, or boiled
  then cooled, or disinfected. Plain clean water works; you do not need to
  add anything to it.
- Get pressure without a kit: poke a pinhole in a bottle cap or in a
  plastic bag, fill with clean water, and squeeze a firm stream (aim for a
  strong squirt, not a trickle). Flush until grit and debris are gone.
- Do NOT scrub dirt in, and do NOT pack plant material down into a deep or
  dirty wound.
- Leave dirty, deep, crush, or animal-bite wounds OPEN. Do not seal them
  shut. Closing them traps bacteria and causes an abscess. Just cover.
- Cover with the cleanest cloth or dressing you have.

== CATTAIL AS DRESSING MATERIAL (empty-kit option) ==
- Seed-head fluff (brown down): soft, absorbent PADDING. Use it as the
  OUTER pad on top of a cleaner inner dressing. It is NOT sterile, despite
  what some sources claim. Never pack it directly into an open wound.
- Gel between the young inner leaves: a clear, sticky mucilage. It soothes
  and forms a film over scrapes, stings, and minor burns. Tradition calls
  it mildly antiseptic, but that is not proven. Treat it as comfort and a
  barrier, NOT a disinfectant. Clean the wound first, every time.
- Mashed rhizome (root) poultice: folk option for a minor sting or scrape
  on intact or barely-broken skin. Same rule: clean first, minor only.
- Split or dried leaves can be improvised as a wrap to hold a dressing on.

== BETTER OPTIONS IF YOU CARRY THEM ==
- Honey: real evidence as an antibacterial dressing, strongest for minor
  wounds and shallow (partial-thickness) burns. Spread a thin layer of
  plain honey over a CLEANED minor wound or small burn, then cover. Do not
  give or apply honey to infants under 1 year (botulism risk). Honey beats
  cattail gel if you have it.
- Any real antiseptic in your kit (povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine) or just
  soap and water cleans far better than any plant.
- Sugar packed on a shallow wound is an old field trick with some basis,
  but honey is cleaner and better if you have a choice.

== MINOR BURNS ==
- Cool it with clean, cool (not ice-cold) water for at least 20 minutes,
  poured or running. This is the single most useful thing you can do, and
  it still helps even if started a bit late.
- Do NOT use ice, butter, oil, or toothpaste. They trap heat or cause harm.
- Do NOT pop blisters. If one tears open on its own, rinse gently and cover.
- Cover loosely with clean cloth. Cattail gel can soothe; honey is better.

== IF YOU CANNOT REACH HELP: MANAGING OVER DAYS ==
- Re-clean and re-flush the wound daily with clean water. Put on the
  cleanest dressing you can find or improvise.
- Keep it covered and reasonably moist, not caked in dirt. Elevate the part
  when you rest.
- Warm, clean, moist compresses a few times a day help the body wall off a
  small local infection and bring a pus pocket to a head.
- Draining is right; sewing infection shut is wrong. If a small, shallow
  abscess forms and comes to a soft point, you can open just the skin over
  the softest spot with a flame-sterilized blade, let it drain, flush it,
  and keep it OPEN to keep draining. Do not dig deep, and leave abscesses
  on the face alone if you possibly can.

== KNOW WHEN IT IS TURNING DANGEROUS (honest limits) ==
- Warning signs, checked daily: spreading redness, growing pain or swelling,
  pus, bad smell, a RED STREAK running up the limb, fever, chills, feeling
  sick all over.
- A red streak plus fever means the infection is entering the body. This can
  progress to sepsis and kill.
- Hard truth: a spreading or deep infection needs antibiotics. No plant, no
  honey, no poultice reliably cures it. Cattail will not. Do not wait on a
  folk remedy while it spreads.
- Best you can do off-grid: keep it draining and flushed, warm compresses,
  rest, elevate, drink water, and start moving toward help.
- Evacuate if you can safely reach help. Deep punctures and dirty wounds
  also carry tetanus risk, which has no field cure. That is another reason
  to reach care when it is reachable, not the only thing you can do now.

== IDENTIFY: CONFIRM BEFORE YOU USE ==
- Full identification is in the edible Cattail entry. Confirm there first.
- Quick check: tall, strap-like leaves rooted in water or muck, and a brown,
  sausage-shaped seed head.

== TOXIC LOOKALIKE: IRIS ==
- IRIS grows in the same wet ground and is TOXIC. Its leaves rise in a FLAT
  FAN, and it NEVER makes a brown sausage head.
- Iris root and sap hold irritant compounds (irisin, iridin) that cause skin
  irritation on contact and vomiting or cramps if any reaches the mouth.
  Never mash an iris root onto broken skin.
- Rule: a flat fan of leaves with no brown seed head means walk away.

== HARVEST SAFELY ==
- Cattails soak up pollutants and heavy metals from their water; they are
  literally used to clean dirty water. Do not harvest from roadside ditches,
  farm or industrial runoff, or any suspect water, even for skin use.

== SEASON ==
- Gel and green growth come spring into summer.
- Seed-head fluff is ready late summer through winter.
