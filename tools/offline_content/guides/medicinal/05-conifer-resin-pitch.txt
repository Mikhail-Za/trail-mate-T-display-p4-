Conifer Resin (Pine, Spruce, Fir Pitch): wound seal

== WHAT IT IS ==
- Sticky sap (pitch) from true pines, spruces, and firs.
- It is mildly antibacterial. Lab and clinical work on
  spruce and pine resin salve shows it slows the growth
  of many bacteria, including tough ones like MRSA, and
  its resin acids can help skin close a wound.
- Best real use in the field: a protective glue-like
  cover and a homemade salve for SMALL, CLEAN wounds,
  cracked skin, and split lips or heels. Topical only.

== WHAT IT CANNOT DO ==
- It does NOT sterilize a wound. It slows bacteria; it
  does not kill them all. It never replaces cleaning.
- It is NOT for deep, dirty, gaping, or infected wounds.
  Sealing those traps bacteria inside and can turn a
  survivable wound into a spreading infection.
- It is not a substitute for stopping bleeding, flushing
  the wound out, or draining an infection.

== DO THIS FIRST (any wound) ==
Work the steps in order. Pitch comes near the end.
1. STOP BLEEDING.
   - Press hard directly on the wound with the cleanest
     cloth you have. Hold 10-15 minutes without peeking.
   - If it soaks through, add more on top, keep pressing.
   - Raise the part above the heart if you can.
   - Spurting or pooling blood from an arm or leg that
     pressure will not stop: tie a tourniquet 2-3 inches
     above the wound (belt, strap, cloth plus a stick to
     twist). Tighten until bleeding stops. Note the time.
     A limb is worth losing to keep a life.
2. CLEAN IT (the step that prevents infection).
   - Scrub the skin AROUND the wound with soap and water.
   - Flush INSIDE the wound with lots of clean water under
     pressure. Volume and pressure matter more than what
     the water is. Use at least a half liter, ideally more.
   - Best water: boiled-and-cooled, filtered, or treated.
     Any clean drinkable water works and is as good as
     sterile saline for this.
   - Make pressure: a squeeze bottle, a hydration bite
     valve, or a plastic bag with a pinhole. Squeeze a
     hard stream to blast out dirt and grit.
   - Pick out any grit, splinters, or dead tissue you can
     reach. Leave tissue you are unsure about.
   - Do NOT pour pitch, sap, alcohol, or fuel INTO the
     wound. Plain water flushing is the goal.
3. DECIDE: close or leave open.
   - CLOSE only if the wound is small, clean, has straight
     edges, less than about 6 hours old, and not a bite,
     puncture, or crush. Pull edges together with tape or
     closure strips, or a thin smear of warmed pitch along
     the OUTSIDE of the closed edges as a glue, then cover.
   - LEAVE OPEN if the wound is deep, dirty, ragged, a bite
     or puncture, older, or you are unsure. Pack loosely
     with clean moist dressing and cover. You can bring the
     edges together later, around day 4, if it stays clean
     (delayed closure). Open-and-draining beats sealed-shut.
4. DRESS AND WATCH.
   - Cover with the cleanest dry cloth you have.
   - Change daily. Re-flush with clean water each change.
   - Inspect every day for infection (see below).

== WHERE PITCH FITS ==
- Use it on a SMALL, CLEANED, bleeding-stopped wound,
  crack, or blister as a protective, mildly antibacterial
  seal, then cover with a clean dressing.
- Use it as a glue to help hold the edges of a small,
  clean cut together from the outside.
- Good for cracked lips, split fingertips, and heels.

== SOURCE AND ID ==
- Use only a true conifer: pine, spruce, or fir. These are
  resinous and ooze sticky pitch at a bark wound.
- Take hardened amber pitch or soft sticky pitch from a
  bark wound or a fallen limb. Do not gouge live trees.
- SIMPLE SAFETY RULE: if it does not ooze sticky pitch,
  it is not your source.
- Avoid YEW (Taxus), a common poisonous lookalike. Yew
  has NO resin and produces no pitch, soft flat needles,
  no cones, a red berry-like fruit, and gnarled red-brown
  bark. All of it except the red flesh is toxic. If a tree
  gives you no sticky pitch, walk away.

== HOW TO USE ==
- Warm a small amount between clean fingers or gently near
  (not in) a fire until it softens. Pitch is flammable;
  keep it away from open flame.
- Smear a THIN layer over the closed, cleaned minor wound
  or skin crack, then cover with a clean dressing.
- SALVE (spreads easier, lasts longer): melt pitch low and
  slow with a little animal fat, tallow, or oil until it
  blends. Let it cool to a soft paste. Store covered.
  Spruce and fir pitch is softer and easier than pine.

== IF IT GETS INFECTED (and you cannot reach care) ==
Signs: growing redness, swelling, warmth, throbbing pain,
pus, or bad smell. WORSE signs: red streaks running up the
limb, tender lumps in the armpit or groin, fever or chills.
Those mean it is spreading and is serious.
Do this, and keep doing it:
- OPEN IT AND LET IT DRAIN. Remove any closure or pitch
  seal. If pus is trapped under tight skin, open the wound
  edge with a flame-cleaned blade so it can drain. A wound
  that drains freely rarely turns deadly. A sealed one can.
- WARM SOAKS. Hold a warm, clean, moist compress on it for
  about 30 minutes, 3 to 4 times a day. This pulls pus out
  and increases blood flow. Re-warm the cloth as it cools.
- FLUSH each time with clean water, then repack loosely and
  cover with a clean dressing. Do NOT re-seal it shut.
- Rest and elevate the part. Drink water. Watch the streaks
  and fever; mark the edge of the redness with ink or mud
  so you can see if it grows.
- HONEST LIMIT: pitch, soaks, and drainage buy time and can
  beat a mild local infection. They do NOT reliably cure a
  spreading infection (cellulitis) or blood infection. There
  is no plant that replaces antibiotics for that. Drainage
  and warm soaks are your best real field tools; use them
  hard while you move toward help.

== HARD TRUTHS (no field substitute) ==
- TETANUS: a dirty or puncture wound in someone not up to
  date on shots is a real risk. There is no field cure. The
  best you can do is flush and clean thoroughly and get a
  booster within a few days if you possibly can.
- Big, deep, or spurting wounds, wounds to the belly, chest,
  or skull, and fast-spreading infection need real medical
  care. Manage aggressively as above, but understand the
  limit.
- Evacuate if you can safely reach help. If you cannot,
  keep cleaning, keep it draining, keep it covered, and
  keep the person warm, fed, and hydrated. Those basics
  save more lives in the field than any single remedy.

== CAUTION ==
- Topical only. Small, clean wounds. Never seal a deep,
  dirty, or infected wound.
- Resin allergy is common. It can cause a red, itchy,
  sometimes blistering rash, usually 1 to 3 days after
  contact. Patch-test a small spot first. If a rash starts,
  wash the pitch off and stop using it.
- Very sticky and hard to remove. Oil or fat loosens it.
- Do NOT put pitch on a fresh or deep burn. Cool a burn
  with clean water first and keep burns clean and covered.

== SEASON ==
- Available year-round. Pitch flows more freely in warm
  weather and hardens in cold.
