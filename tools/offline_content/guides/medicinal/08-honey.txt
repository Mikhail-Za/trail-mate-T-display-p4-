Honey: field wound and burn dressing

== WHY HONEY ==
- Not a plant, but a classic, well-established wound remedy worth carrying.
- Raw honey is naturally antibacterial. Its high sugar, low water, mild acidity, and a trace of hydrogen peroxide all discourage bacteria. Modern medicine still uses honey dressings.
- On the skin: a thin coat over a minor wound, scrape, or minor burn, then a cover. It slows bacteria and keeps the dressing from sticking.

== BEST FOR ==
- Minor burns and scalds (once cooled), blisters, shallow scrapes, and chapped or cracked lips.
- It works best on shallow, clean wounds you can keep covered.

== HOW TO USE ==
- Clean the wound and control bleeding first.
- For a minor burn, cool it with clean water for several minutes first, then apply honey.
- Smear a thin layer of honey over the minor wound or minor burn.
- Cover with a clean, non-stick dressing. Change it daily or when it soaks through.
- Use plain raw or pure honey. Flavored or heavily processed honey with additives is not the same thing.

== CAUTION ==
- NEVER give honey to a baby under 1 year old, by mouth OR on broken skin. Risk of infant botulism. This is absolute.
- Topical use for minor wounds and burns only. Deep, large, or dirty wounds and serious burns need real care. Clean and evacuate.
- Allergy to honey or bee products is rare but possible. Stop if irritation gets worse.
- Honey is a comfort and protective measure, not a cure for infection. Watch for spreading redness, pus, red streaks, or fever, and evacuate (see the first-aid section).

== NOTE ==
- Medical-grade or manuka honey is ideal, but any pure honey works in a pinch.
