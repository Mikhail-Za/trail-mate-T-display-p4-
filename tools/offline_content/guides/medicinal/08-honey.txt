Honey: field wound and burn dressing

== WHY IT WORKS ==
- Honey is not a plant, but it is a proven wound and burn
  dressing and it keeps forever, so it earns its weight.
- Four things fight bacteria at once: high sugar and low water
  pull moisture OUT of germs, mild acidity (pH 3 to 4.5) is
  hostile to them, and diluted honey releases a trace of
  hydrogen peroxide.
- It also keeps the dressing from sticking, keeps the wound
  moist so it heals faster, and lifts away dead tissue and gunk.
- This is real medicine, not folklore. Hospitals use honey
  dressings, and good evidence shows honey heals shallow burns
  several days faster than standard dressings.

== USE IT FIRST: WOUNDS ==
Do these steps in order. This is the whole treatment.
- 1. Stop bleeding: firm direct pressure until it stops.
- 2. Flush hard: rinse the wound with a LOT of clean or
     treated water, under pressure. Improvise a jet by poking a
     pinhole in a filled zip bag or bottle and squeezing.
     Getting dirt and germs OUT matters more than anything.
- 3. Pick out any visible debris you can reach.
- 4. Smear a layer of honey right over the wound, thicker than
     you think. It can fill a shallow cavity.
- 5. Cover with the cleanest cloth or dressing you have.
- 6. Re-do it daily, or sooner if it soaks through. Flush again
     each change, re-honey, re-cover.
- Best on shallow, cleanable wounds: scrapes, cuts, blisters,
  cracked skin and lips. Use plain raw or pure honey. Flavored
  or additive honey is not the same thing.

== USE IT FIRST: BURNS ==
- 1. Stop the burning. Remove hot clothing and any ring or
     watch before the area swells.
- 2. COOL IT: run clean cool (not ice cold) water over the burn
     for about 20 minutes. Best within 3 hours of the burn.
     Use water, never ice, which deepens the injury.
- 3. In cold or wet conditions, cool only the burn and keep the
     rest of the body warm. A big burn plus cold water can drop
     the whole body into dangerous hypothermia. Weigh that risk.
- 4. Leave blisters INTACT. Skin over a blister is a sterile
     dressing. Do not pop or peel it in the field.
- 5. Once cooled, smear honey over the burn and cover loosely
     with a clean non-stick dressing.
- 6. Change daily and re-honey. Watch for infection.
- Wilderness burn guidelines specifically allow honey (or
  antibiotic ointment) on deeper burns when you are managing in
  the field or evacuation is delayed. That is exactly your case.

== IF YOU CANNOT REACH HELP ==
No way out for days? Honey plus discipline is your infection
plan. You are trying to buy time and keep germs from spreading.
- Keep the wound covered and moist with honey at all times.
- Flush generously at EVERY dressing change, not just the first.
- Redress at least once a day. A soaked or dirty dressing is
  worse than none.
- Rest and elevate the injured part. Movement pumps infection
  outward.
- Keep drinking water and keep eating. Your body heals the
  wound; you have to fuel it.

If infection sets in (redness spreading past the wound, heat,
swelling, pus, bad smell, or fever), escalate:
- 1. Open and DRAIN any pocket of pus. Trapped pus must get out.
- 2. Apply a warm (not scalding) compress several times a day to
     help pus drain and ease pain.
- 3. Flush the opened wound hard, pack with honey, cover, repeat
     2 to 3 times a day.
- 4. Mark the edge of the redness with ink, charcoal, or a
     scratch. Check hourly whether it is growing.
- 5. Take oral antibiotics IF you carry them. This is the main
     reason to pack them.

== OTHER SURVIVAL USES ==
- Cough and sore throat: a spoonful coats the throat and calms
  a cough as well as or better than drugstore syrup. Adults
  1 to 2 teaspoons as needed; children over 1 year, half to one
  teaspoon. Never under age 1.
- Quick energy: honey is fast fuel, about 60 calories per
  tablespoon, useful for cold, exhaustion, or a shaky low-blood-
  sugar feeling.
- Rehydration drink: stir a few teaspoons of honey plus a
  half teaspoon of salt into a liter of clean water to replace
  what is lost to diarrhea, vomiting, or heavy sweat.
- Chapped, cracked, or sunburned skin and lips: a thin coat
  soothes and protects.
- Storage: pure honey does not spoil. Crystallized honey is
  fine; warm it gently to re-liquefy.

== HARD LIMITS: BE HONEST ==
Honey lowers infection risk and buys time. It does NOT replace
these, and pretending otherwise gets people killed:
- Big, deep, or full-thickness burns (leathery, white, charred,
  or painless skin), or burns to the face, hands, feet,
  genitals, or circling a limb: honey helps the surface only.
  The real dangers are fluid loss, infection, and hypothermia.
  Keep covered, keep warm, push fluids, evacuate if you can.
- Deep, gaping, crushed, or heavily contaminated wounds, and
  anything from an animal bite or a dirty puncture: honey does
  not prevent tetanus or rabies. There is no field substitute
  for the tetanus shot, rabies shots, or stitching a deep wound.
- Spreading infection with fever, red streaks running up a limb,
  or feeling very sick: that is the infection going body-wide
  (sepsis). Without antibiotics it can kill within days. Honey,
  drainage, and flushing slow it but do NOT cure it. Do not wait
  on a folk remedy. This is the moment to get out at all costs.
- Evacuate to real care whenever you safely can. Self-treatment
  is what you do because help is not reachable, not instead of
  reaching it.

== ABSOLUTE RULES ==
- NEVER give honey to a baby under 1 year, by mouth OR on broken
  skin. Risk of infant botulism. No exceptions.
- Raw honey is not sterile. Medical-grade or manuka honey is
  better if you have it, but for an older child or adult any pure
  honey beats an open, unprotected wound.
- Bee-product allergy is rare but real. Stop if irritation gets
  worse instead of better.
